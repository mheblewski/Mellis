*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Elbarbary N, Moser O, Al Yaarubi S, Alsaffar H, Al Shaikh A, Ajjan RA, Deeb A. Use of continuous glucose monitoring trend arrows in the younger population with type 1 diabetes. Diab Vasc Dis Res 2021;18(6):14791641211062155, https://doi.org/10.1177/14791641211062155.*

## Key points

- Narrative review and expert consensus (Arab Society for Paediatric Endocrinology and Diabetes with international advisors) on using CGM trend arrows in young people; there is no standardised evidence-based protocol [Abstract; Day-to-day use].
- Arrow meanings differ between CGM brands; know the device's own scale [Table 1].
- Dose adjustments by arrow and insulin sensitivity are added to the ICR/correction-factor calculation, never used instead of it; a paediatric Dexcom version adds a 75 to < 125 mg/dL sensitivity band [Table 2].
- Children and adolescents should wait at least 3 h after a meal dose before a further correction to avoid stacking [Day-to-day use].
- Straight up/down arrows before a meal were linked to more than double the risk of post-meal high/low respectively [Trend arrows section].
- Exercise: pause at least temporarily below 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L), especially with a falling arrow; starchy snack at or below 80 mg/dL (4.4 mmol/L) after exercise [Exercise].
- Bedtime target about 130 mg/dL (7.2 mmol/L) with a flat or up-angled arrow; be very careful with extra insulin near bedtime [Nocturnal hypoglycaemia].

## Trend arrows across CGM systems (Table 1)

Rates in mg/dL/min (mmol/L/min); arrows indicate the likely direction over the next 60+ minutes.
- FreeStyle Libre 1/2: ↑ > 2 (> 0.11); ↗ 1–2 (0.06–0.11); → < 1 (< 0.06); ↘ 1–2; ↓ > 2.
- Dexcom G5/G6: ↑↑ > 3 (0.17); ↑ 2–3 (0.11–0.17); ↗ 1–2; → under 1; ↘ 1–2; ↓ 2–3; ↓↓ > 3.
- Medtronic (Veo/640G): single arrow 1–2, double 2–3, triple > 3 in either direction.
- Eversense: ↑ or ↓ > 2; ↗ or ↘ 1–2; → 0–1.

Example: at a steady 2 mg/dL/min (0.1 mmol/L/min) glucose would rise about 60 mg/dL (3.3 mmol/L) in 30 min. Interstitial readings lag blood glucose by about 4–15 min, more when glucose is rising fast (≥ 3 mg/dL/min, ≥ 0.2 mmol/L/min) [Trend arrows section].

Sensitivity bands and age (paediatric Dexcom guidance): ≥ 125 mg/dL toddlers/pre-school; 75–125 young school age; 50–75 older school age; 25–50 early puberty; < 25 pubertal teens [Day-to-day use].

## Corrections, stacking and meal doses

- On injections, aim for post-meal glucose ≤ 162 mg/dL (≤ 9.0 mmol/L), back to target within 2–4 h. Rising arrows 2–4 h after a meal suggest the bolus was too small; falling arrows suggest a smaller dose or extra carbohydrate [Day-to-day use].
- Before changing a dose, weigh the size and timing of the last insulin, recent food, exercise and time of day.
- Wait at least 3 h after a meal dose before a correction (children and adolescents).
- A bedtime snack may be needed when glucose is in the lower target range with a downward arrow.
- After raising a dose, check hourly; after lowering it or taking carbs, check even more often.
- Arrows show a trend, not an exact value; the right action differs between people and situations.

## Nocturnal hypoglycaemia and sick days

- Children are prone to night lows (deep sleep, play, blunted counter-regulation). A downward arrow at bedtime calls for an early recheck or carbohydrate, depending on the level [Nocturnal hypoglycaemia].
- Sick days: monitor more often; corrections may be considered about every 2–3 h, guided by glucose and arrows; test ketones more often, especially with medicines that can disturb some sensors (acetaminophen with some devices, high-dose ascorbic acid) [Sick days].

## Exercise

- Aerobic low-to-moderate exercise raises hypoglycaemia risk for 24 h or longer; intense or anaerobic exercise can raise glucose acutely but still add night-time risk. Intensity check: talking easily = low, talking with effort = moderate, cannot talk = intense [Exercise].
- Set CGM alerts more sensitively around exercise. Stop at least temporarily below 90 mg/dL (5.0 mmol/L), especially with a falling arrow; restart at ≥ 90 mg/dL with an upward arrow; recheck at least 30 min after carbohydrate [Exercise; Figure 1 notes].
- With glucose > 270 mg/dL (> 15.0 mmol/L) and blood ketones > 1.5 mmol/L only mild aerobic exercise may continue; recheck ketones after exercise [Figure 1 notes].
- After exercise: monitor at least 2 h, and 6–8 h after long aerobic sessions; starchy snack at ≤ 80 mg/dL (4.4 mmol/L); for fast rises or very high values a correction of 50% of the usual dose may be considered [Exercise].
- Avoid corrections close to bedtime after exercise; set the night low alert higher; intermittent-scan users scan once or twice overnight (e.g. 1 a.m. and 3 a.m.).

## CGM accuracy and lag

- CGM lag vs blood was about 5–10 min during rises; newer sensors average under 2.5 min. MARD below 10% across the glucose range is generally accepted as clinically safe [Differences in calculation].
- During prolonged exercise, older Dexcom sensors lagged over 10 min with MARD rising to about 13%; use fingersticks if a low is suspected.
- Glucose may swing more after a hypoglycaemic episode.
- Menstrual cycle: higher hyperglycaemia risk in the first half, a trend to more lows in the second half [Menstruation]. Ramadan fasting with trained CGM use was feasible in a pilot of 51 children without severe lows or DKA [Ramadan].

## Limits of this summary

- Children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes on injections or pumps; nothing specific to automated insulin delivery.
- Narrative review and expert opinion; the dose tables are reproduced from adult and paediatric source papers, and one adult column appears to contain a transcription error.
- Figure 1 (exercise flow chart) is covered only through its notes; Ramadan and education sections are shortened.
- Read the original and the primary sources before clinical use.
