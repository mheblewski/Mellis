*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Wolpert HA, Atakov-Castillo A, Smith SA, Steil GM. Dietary fat acutely increases glucose concentrations and insulin requirements in patients with type 1 diabetes: implications for carbohydrate-based bolus dose calculation and intensive diabetes management. Diabetes Care 2013;36(4):810-816, https://doi.org/10.2337/dc12-0092.*

## Key points

- Two dinners with the same carbohydrate and protein but 60 g versus 10 g fat were compared in 7 adults with type 1 diabetes on a research closed-loop system. The high-fat (HF) dinner needed more insulin: 12.6 ± 1.9 vs 9.0 ± 1.3 units (P = 0.01) [Abstract; Results].
- Even with that extra insulin, glucose stayed higher after the HF dinner: area above 120 mg/dL (6.7 mmol/L) was about twice as large (16,967 vs 8,350 mg/dL·min, P < 0.0001) [Results].
- The effective carbohydrate-to-insulin ratio for the HF dinner was lower (stronger), about 9 g/unit versus 13 g/unit for the low-fat (LF) dinner (P = 0.01) [Results].
- On average, insulin need rose by 42% with the HF dinner, but individuals ranged from −17% to +108%. The size of the rise correlated with daily insulin requirement (R² = 0.64, P = 0.03), not with BMI and not with the person's usual carb ratio [Results; Conclusions].
- The extra insulin was needed late: insulin levels were higher roughly 5 to 10 hours after the meal (11 pm to 4 am window) [Results].
- The authors conclude that carb counting alone underestimates the dose for higher-fat meals and that alternative dosing algorithms are needed; they do not propose a validated formula [Conclusions].

## Study design (crossover, closed loop)

- Adults aged 21-70 with type 1 diabetes for more than 5 years, A1C below 9%, pump users for more than 6 months; 11 studies run, 7 analysed (5 men, 2 women; mean age 55, A1C 7.2%, total daily dose about 0.5 U/kg) [Research Design and Methods; Results].
- Each person had two 18-hour closed-loop periods starting at dinner. Dinners had identical carbohydrate (about 96 g) and protein; only fat differed (10 g vs 60 g, mostly saturated fat). Lunches and the following breakfasts were identical and low in fat, and afternoon activity was matched [Diet intervention; Study design considerations].
- The closed-loop algorithm (proportional-integral-derivative type, target 110 mg/dL) gave a small priming bolus about 15 minutes before each meal and then dosed automatically. Insulin needed for each dinner was counted as the priming bolus plus closed-loop insulin from 6 pm to 11 pm [Closed-loop control system; Statistical analysis].
- Hypoglycaemia was actively avoided (low alarms at 90 mg/dL or higher, carbohydrate given ahead of any impending low); no glucose of 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L) or lower occurred [Study design considerations; Results].

## Fat, delayed hyperglycaemia and insulin resistance

- The high glucose after the HF dinner developed over several hours, which fits the known effect of free fatty acids (FFAs) impairing insulin sensitivity and raising liver glucose output, on top of slower gastric emptying [Introduction; Conclusions].
- The two identical breakfasts the next mornings gave similar glucose and insulin profiles, which supports the view that the dinner difference came from fat [Results; Conclusions].
- Earlier pizza studies cited by the authors had found that an extended bolus, with or without a higher total dose, reduces late post-meal highs [Introduction].

## Implications for bolus calculation

- Because responses vary a lot between people, the authors suggest reviewing each person's food and CGM records to see whether highs follow higher-fat meals, and coaching on lower-fat swaps for problem foods [Conclusions].
- They mention the fat-protein unit approach (extra insulin based on fat and protein, calculated from the carb ratio) as not yet validated in a crossover study; in their data the carb ratio did not predict how much extra insulin the HF meal needed [Conclusions].
- They expect food insulin index (FII) dosing to underestimate insulin for higher-fat meals, because FII is based on the first 2 hours after eating [Conclusions].
- The result also matters for AID systems: post-meal highs here partly reflect closed-loop limits such as sensor lag and delayed insulin action [Conclusions].

## Limits of this summary

- Very small adult study (n = 7, mean age 55); the authors state that children, adolescents and other groups need separate study.
- The HF dinner also had more calories, the fat was mostly saturated, and the design could not show a dose-response to the amount of fat.
- The closed-loop system was a research algorithm from 2012, not Trio, Loop or a commercial AID; methods detail and figures are left out here.
- No dosing rule is given by the source; read the original for any clinical decision.
