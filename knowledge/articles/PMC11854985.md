International Society for Pediatric and Adolescent Diabetes Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2024: Diabetes Technologies: Glucose Monitoring. Horm Res Paediatr. 2024;97(6):615-635, https://doi.org/10.1159/000543156.*

## Key points

- Continual CGM use is strongly recommended for all children, adolescents and young adults with T1D [grade A]; starting it soon after diagnosis may improve HbA1c in the first year and later [grade B] [Benefits of CGM].
- People using CGM still need a blood glucose meter (BGM) [grade A] — for calibration if required, suspected wrong readings, fast-changing glucose, the **sensor warm-up** period, signal loss, and warning messages [Capillary Blood Glucose Monitoring].
- New in 2024: rtCGM is now linked to **fewer DKA episodes and fewer severe hypoglycaemic events** than BGM alone [grade B]; rtCGM is rated better than first-generation isCGM [grade A] [Benefits of CGM].
- Warm-up times and MARD differ by sensor: e.g. Dexcom G7 30 min warm-up, MARD about 8–9%; Dexcom G6 2 h, about 8–10%; FreeStyle Libre 3 1 h, 7.5% (adults) / 8.6% (children); Guardian 4 2 h, 10.6% / 11.6% [Table 2].
- CGM and blood glucose differ most during hypoglycaemia and fast changes, because of physiological lag, sensor response, smoothing and motion/pressure. Home-use accuracy can be worse than in studies [Accuracy of CGM Devices].
- CGM users should be taught which substances can disturb readings [grade E]: hydroxyurea, acetaminophen/paracetamol (sensor-dependent dose), high-dose vitamin C, salicylic acid, some cardiac and asthma drugs [Sensor Interference].
- An ambulatory glucose profile (AGP) needs at least **14 days with >70% wear** to show reliable patterns [grade C] [CGM Interpretation and Analysis].
- CGM metrics include TIR 70–180 mg/dL (3.9–10.0 mmol/L), GMI and, new, **time in tight range** 70–140 mg/dL (3.9–7.8 mmol/L) [Continuous Glucose Monitoring].
- Glycaemic benefit needs consistent wear, more than 75% of the time [Education].

## What is new vs the 2022 chapter

- Stronger emphasis on CGM from diagnosis, and possible use in youth with type 2 diabetes on insulin [grade B].
- More weight on ongoing education for families and health professionals, including data interpretation.
- A detailed comparison table of current sensors (ages, factory calibration, non-adjunctive use, wear time, warm-up, MARD).
- New evidence: fewer DKA and severe hypoglycaemia episodes with CGM; quality-of-life benefits. The 2022 chapter called registry results on severe hypoglycaemia inconsistent; 2024 says recent registry analyses show a reduction, visible from the first year of use.
- New graded statements on exercise, skin, remote monitoring, telemedicine, and AGP interpretation.
- New: CGM for screening and staging of early (pre-symptomatic) T1D; "time in tight range" as a metric; alcohol, cannabis and stimulants added to the list of times to test.
- Interference section updated with sensor-specific notes (paracetamol on Guardian at standard doses vs Dexcom G6/G7 above 1000 mg every 6 h) and alcohol (effect not clinically relevant) [Summary of what is new; Sensor Interference].

## Blood glucose meters (BGM) and when to test

- Use only meters meeting ISO 15197:2013 or FDA standards [grade E]; real-world accuracy can be lower than in testing.
- Accuracy can be affected by temperature, humidity, oxygen level and substances such as uric acid, acetaminophen, L-dopa, ascorbic acid and tolazamide; hands must be washed and dried, strips in date and from reliable sources [Meter Standards and Accuracy].
- Without CGM, 6–10 checks a day may be needed [grade B]. Each extra daily check was linked to about 0.2% lower HbA1c on MDI and 0.1% on pumps [Frequency and Timing of BGM].
- Test before meals and snacks, after meals (e.g. 2–3 h), at bedtime and at night, before/during/after exercise, when a low or high is suspected, during and after alcohol (and cannabis or stimulants, which may also raise DKA risk), before critical tasks such as driving, and during illness [grade B].
- With limited resources: at least before meals and at bedtime; testing 3–4 times on the same day several days a week gives more information than one check a day [grade E].
- Bolus-advisor meters increased the share of people at HbA1c target and reduced hypoglycaemia in RCTs [Expert Meters].

## Sensor types, warm-up and calibration

- rtCGM sends values automatically to a pump, phone or receiver, with alerts and cloud sharing; isCGM gives values when scanned (first generation without alarms, second with optional alarms); masked CGM is mainly for research [Table 1].
- FreeStyle Libre 2 used with the phone app streams values continuously, so it works like rtCGM.
- Factory-calibrated: Dexcom G6, G7, Guardian 4, Simplera, FreeStyle Libre 1/2/3. Manual calibration remains an option on some rtCGM when sensor and meter disagree for a long time. For sensors needing calibration, calibrate when glucose is stable (no trend arrow), e.g. three times a day if every 12 h is required [Categories of Sensors; Table 3].

## CGM accuracy, MARD and lag

- Differences between CGM and blood glucose are common during hypoglycaemia or rapid change. Causes: delay of glucose moving into interstitial fluid, sensor response time, signal smoothing, and biomechanical factors (motion, **pressure** — the cause of compression lows) [Accuracy of CGM Devices].
- Accuracy is measured with MARD, error grids or agreement rates. MARD depends on study design: studies with less glucose variability report lower MARD than real-life studies; paediatric MARD often differs from adult MARD.
- There are still no international minimum accuracy standards for CGM apart from the FDA iCGM class; IFCC and CLSI are working on them.

## Sensor interference

- **Hydroxyurea** (therapeutic doses): markedly high sensor readings, e.g. Dexcom G6/G7 and Medtronic Guardian.
- **Acetaminophen/paracetamol:** falsely high on Medtronic Guardian at standard doses; on Dexcom G6/G7 at doses above 1000 mg every 6 h.
- **Salicylic acid:** may slightly lower readings.
- **Ascorbic acid (vitamin C)** at supra-therapeutic doses: falsely high (e.g. FreeStyle Libre).
- Lisinopril, albuterol and atenolol may interfere.
- **Alcohol** can affect accuracy, probably via pH changes, but not to a clinically relevant degree.
- Interference depends on brand and sensor coating. Over-the-counter products may contain these substances without the user knowing. Check with a meter when symptoms do not match the CGM [Sensor Interference; grade E].

## When to use a fingerstick (CGM and AID users)

Even with factory-calibrated, non-adjunctive CGM and AID, a meter is needed to confirm hypoglycaemia, when readings seem wrong or do not fit symptoms, when glucose is changing fast, during warm-up, during signal gaps or warning messages, and to calibrate if needed [Capillary Blood Glucose Monitoring; Conclusions].

## CGM metrics and interpretation

- Metrics: TIR (70–180 mg/dL, 3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time below range (TBR), time above range (TAR), coefficient of variation (%CV), standard deviation, GMI (estimated HbA1c from CGM), time in tight range (70–140 mg/dL, 3.9–7.8 mmol/L). They complement HbA1c; evidence for some is still evolving [Continuous Glucose Monitoring].
- AGP: at least 14 consecutive days with ≥70% data capture [grade C].
- Suggested review steps: confirm how long CGM was active; look at TIR, TBR, TAR, %CV, GMI; set personal goals; agree a stepwise plan. Use non-judgemental, strength-based language [CGM Interpretation and Analysis].
- In autoantibody-positive youth, spending ≥5–10% of time above 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) was linked to progression to stage 3 T1D; CGM is not yet a full replacement for OGTT in trials [CGM Use for Screening].

## Evidence on benefit

- rtCGM lowers HbA1c, raises TIR, improves quality of life, reduces mild-to-moderate hypoglycaemia and time low, across all insulin therapies; benefit depends on wear time [grade A].
- A meta-analysis found rtCGM gave 7% more TIR and 1.7% less time below 70 mg/dL than first-generation isCGM, with no HbA1c difference; second-generation isCGM vs rtCGM has not been compared directly [Comparing rtCGM and isCGM].
- isCGM: 11–13 scans/day go with better HbA1c and TIR, though not less time below range in first-generation studies [grade B].
- Starting CGM early after diagnosis: about 0.7% (7.5 mmol/mol) lower HbA1c at 12 months, better HbA1c over 7 years, fewer severe lows [CGM Use from Diabetes Onset].
- In young children (mean 5.7 years), rtCGM reduced hypoglycaemia without a significant TIR change.

## Alerts, exercise, remote monitoring and skin

- Alerts at start (Table 3): consider leaving them off at first; then personalised, wide thresholds such as 70–250 mg/dL (3.9–13.9 mmol/L); add rate-of-change or predictive alerts later or for situations with faster changes such as more activity. Educate on **sensor lag** and **trend arrows**, and agree a remote-monitoring plan.
- **Exercise:** CGM can reduce glucose swings with activity [grade C]; rate of change and trend arrows help predict lows before, during and after activity [grade E]. Low thresholds plus arrows help decide when to take carbohydrate; remote monitoring helps carers act and prevent post-exercise night lows [Exercise].
- **Remote monitoring** improves parents' quality of life, stress and sleep and can prevent prolonged night lows [grade E]. Agree "rules of engagement" with teenagers, or they may switch sharing off [Remote Monitoring; Quality of Life].
- **Skin:** irritation and allergy (often acrylate adhesives) are barriers, especially in young and atopic children; use correct placement, skin care, barriers, careful removal, and dermatology patch testing if allergy is suspected [grade E].

## Limits of this summary

- Population: children, adolescents and young adults, mostly with T1D; device data reflect 2024 and vary by country.
- Grades range from A (CGM benefit) to E (practical advice); sensor MARD values come from different study designs and are not directly comparable.
- Psychosocial, T2D and screening sections are shortened.
- For clinical decisions, read the original guideline and the device instructions.
