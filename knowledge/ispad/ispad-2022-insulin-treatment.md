*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Cengiz E, Danne T, Ahmad T, et al. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Insulin treatment in children and adolescents with diabetes. Pediatric Diabetes 2022;23:1277–1296, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13442.*

## Key points

- Start insulin as soon as possible after diagnosis, usually within 6 hours when ketonuria is present, to prevent decompensation and DKA (grade A). [Executive summary, p. 1277]
- Intensive basal-bolus therapy, by multiple daily injections (MDI) or pump, is the gold standard at all ages (E); therapy must be individualised (E) and backed by age-appropriate education (A). [Executive summary, p. 1277]
- Prandial insulin given before the meal works better than insulin given after it and is preferred where possible (C). [Executive summary, p. 1278]
- Rapid-acting analogues (aspart, lispro, glulisine): onset about 0.15–0.35 h, peak 1–3 h, duration 3–5 h. Faster aspart: onset 0.1–0.2 h, peak 1–3 h, duration 3–5 h. Larger doses tend to act longer. [Table 1, p. 1279]
- Ideal timing for rapid-acting analogues is 10–15 min before the meal (or immediately before); 15–20 min may be needed for full effect, especially at breakfast; give the dose ahead of eating when glucose is already high. [Section 4.1.2, p. 1279–1280; Section 5.1, p. 1283]
- Starting ratios: "500 rule" for the insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio (500 ÷ total daily dose = grams covered by 1 unit), with 330 or 250 used in preschool children; "1800 rule" for the correction factor in mg/dL (1500 for more insulin-resistant groups) and "100 rule" in mmol/L. [Section 6, p. 1284–1285]
- Typical total daily dose: <0.5 U/kg/day in partial remission, 0.7–1.0 U/kg/day before puberty, 1–2 U/kg/day during puberty. [Section 6, p. 1284]
- Prandial insulin is roughly 55–70% and basal 30–45% of the daily dose on injection regimens (basal 30–50% in the dosage section). [Section 5.1, p. 1283; Section 6, p. 1284]
- Doses should follow the daily glucose pattern (B) and be reviewed regularly, since needs differ between people and change over time (E). [Executive summary, p. 1278]
- Storage: unused insulin at 2–8 °C; insulin in use at room temperature (below 25–30 °C) for up to 4 weeks; discard insulin that has been frozen or looks abnormal. [Section 7.3, p. 1289]

## Rapid-acting insulin pharmacology (onset, peak, duration)

Table 1 groups insulins approved for children into prandial, intermediate and basal types. Values are for subcutaneous use and given in hours [Table 1, p. 1279]:

| Insulin | Onset (h) | Peak (h) | Duration (h) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Faster aspart (ultra-rapid) | 0.1–0.2 | 1–3 | 3–5 |
| Aspart, lispro, glulisine (rapid-acting analogues) | 0.15–0.35 | 1–3 | 3–5 |
| Regular / soluble (short-acting) | 0.5–1 | 2–4 | 5–8 |
| NPH (intermediate) | 2–4 | 4–12 | 12–24 |
| Glargine U100 | 2–4 | 8–12 | 22–24 |
| Detemir | 1–2 | 4–7 | 20–24 |
| Glargine U300 | 2–6 | minimal peak | 30–36 |
| Degludec | 0.5–1.5 | minimal peak | >42 |

The table note says dose matters: big doses last longer than small ones, and the listed durations of NPH and the basal analogues may be shorter in practice [Table 1].

**Rapid-acting analogues (RAI).** One unit of an analogue lowers glucose as much as one unit of regular insulin; only the timing differs. The three analogues are approved for children: lispro at any age, aspart from 1 year, glulisine from 6 years. No clinically important difference in onset or duration has been shown between them [Section 4.1.2, p. 1279]. Analogues bring high glucose down faster than regular insulin, with or without ketosis, and are the insulin used in pumps [Section 4.1.2, p. 1280].

**Ultra-rapid insulins.** Faster aspart contains niacinamide and L-arginine, which speed up the break-up of hexamers into absorbable monomers. Onset and offset are quicker than aspart, with a smaller early post-meal spike and less hypoglycaemia some hours later. It is approved from 1 year (EMA) and from 2 years (FDA). In a 26-week trial in 1–18-year-olds (with degludec), faster aspart given at or after the meal worked as well as aspart with no new safety signal. Ultra-rapid lispro (URLi) was approved only for adults at the time of writing; a small meal study had been done in 6–18-year-olds, and the chapter gives no separate onset/peak table values for it. Ultra-rapid insulins may be especially useful in pumps and AID systems. Inhaled human insulin acts fastest but was not approved for children. [Section 4.1.3, p. 1280]

**Regular insulin** is still essential in many countries. As a pre-meal bolus it should be given 20–30 min before eating [Section 4.1.1, p. 1279; Section 5.1, p. 1283].

**Basal insulins, briefly.** NPH needs twice-daily dosing and fixed meals; its evening dose peaks around midnight to 4 am, when needs are lowest (night-time hypoglycaemia risk), and fades by early morning (dawn hyperglycaemia); it must be rolled to resuspend. Glargine gives about 24 h without a clear peak; detemir often needs two doses a day in children; glargine U300 needs 3–5 days to reach full effect; degludec has a half-life of about 25 h, so changes are made every 3–4 days, and in adults it can be given at any time of day if at least 8 h have passed since the last injection. In a paediatric trial degludec gave equal HbA1c with 30% less basal insulin than detemir and less hyperglycaemia with ketosis. Weekly insulin (icodec) had no paediatric data. [Sections 4.2–4.3, p. 1280–1282]

**Premixed insulins** are not routinely recommended for children because they remove the freedom to adjust prandial and basal doses separately; higher DKA and severe hypoglycaemia rates have been reported with them [Section 4.4, p. 1282].

## Bolus timing

- Ideally 10–15 min before meals or right before them; earlier dosing lowers post-meal glucose and may also reduce night-time hypoglycaemia [Section 4.1.2, p. 1279].
- Giving rapid-acting analogues 15 min before the meal led to smaller post-meal excursions and more time in 3.5–10.0 mmol/L without more hypoglycaemia [Section 6.2, p. 1285].
- If glucose is high before the meal, give the analogue in advance of eating [Section 4.1.2, p. 1280].
- For erratic eaters (for example young children), dosing after the meal is an exceptional option to match the dose to what was actually eaten and limit hypoglycaemia; children with predictable eating do better with pre-meal dosing [Section 4.1.2, p. 1279].
- Ultra-rapid analogues may be given closer to the meal than standard analogues [Section 5.1, p. 1283].
- Missed meal boluses are a major cause of poor glycaemia: missing more than one meal injection a week raises HbA1c by about 0.3–0.8% [Section 6.2, p. 1285].

## Insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio (ICR) and insulin sensitivity factor (ISF)

The chapter treats bolus calculation from carbohydrate amount and glucose as the core of intensive therapy [Section 6, p. 1284].

- **500 rule (ICR):** 500 divided by the total daily dose (basal + bolus) gives the grams of carbohydrate one unit of rapid-acting insulin covers. It is a starting point: breakfast often needs more insulin, a meal just before or after exercise less. In toddlers and preschoolers a 330 or 250 rule (50–100% more insulin) may be used instead. Ratios should then be checked against real meals. [Section 6, p. 1284]
- **Meal-specific ICR:** dividing the meal's carbohydrate grams by the insulin units that worked for it often gives the most accurate ratio for that meal, especially breakfast, when insulin resistance is usually higher. If glucose before and after the meal differs by more than 2–3 mmol/L (36–54 mg/dL), the correction factor can show how much more or less insulin that meal needed. [Section 6, p. 1285]
- **Fat and protein** also change insulin needs and should be considered when choosing bolus doses (details in the ISPAD nutrition chapter). [Section 6, p. 1285]
- **Correction factor / ISF:** "1800 rule" — 1800 ÷ total daily dose = mg/dL drop per unit of rapid-acting insulin; 1500 ÷ total daily dose for more insulin-resistant groups, and the 1500 rule may be used when correcting with regular insulin. In mmol/L, the "100 rule" — 100 ÷ total daily dose. [Section 6, p. 1285]
- **Correction with meals:** add a correction dose to the prandial dose when pre-meal glucose is above target. A correction bolus of rapid-acting insulin can also be given between meals. [Section 6.1.2, p. 1285; Section 4.1, p. 1279]

## Total daily dose by age and puberty

Insulin needs depend on age, weight, puberty stage, diabetes duration and phase, injection sites, food, exercise, routine, glucose results, illness and menstrual cycle [Section 6, p. 1284].

- Partial remission ("honeymoon"), usually starting weeks after diagnosis: typically <0.5 U/kg/day; its appearance calls for prompt, large dose reductions to avoid hypoglycaemia [Section 6, p. 1284; Section 6.1.1, p. 1285].
- Prepubertal children outside remission: usually 0.7–1.0 U/kg/day.
- Puberty: may rise to 1–2 U/kg/day, partly due to growth-hormone-driven insulin resistance.
- Glucose may be higher in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle (progesterone). [Section 6, p. 1284]
- Basal share on basal-bolus regimens: 30–50% of the total daily dose. Switching to glargine: reduce total basal by about 20%. Switching from glargine to degludec: reported doses range 100–150% of the glargine dose. Moving from degludec to a pump: reduce pump basal by 20% for the first 1–2 days. [Section 6, p. 1284]

## Dose adjustment principles

- Use daily or weekly patterns from SMBG or CGM, and teach families to make adjustments themselves [Section 6.1, p. 1285].
- Soon after diagnosis, adjust frequently (many centres daily) [Section 6.1.1, p. 1285].
- Basal (long-acting) dose is titrated on overnight and fasting glucose; post-meal control depends on well-timed prandial insulin with enough coverage for the food. Check glucose at the prandial insulin peak, 1.5–2 h after the injection, to judge the meal dose. [Section 6.1.2, p. 1285]
- Persistent patterns [Section 6.2, p. 1285]:
  - high before breakfast → raise the evening or bedtime intermediate/long-acting dose (check overnight glucose to make sure it causes no night hypoglycaemia);
  - high after a meal → raise that meal's prandial dose;
  - high before lunch or dinner → raise morning basal or breakfast prandial insulin, after ruling out snacks eaten without insulin; on analogues, a rise several hours after a meal points to basal dose or type, because analogues do most of their work within 2–3 h;
  - persistent post-meal highs with carbohydrate counting → review the ICR; persistent highs after correction doses → review the ISF.
- Unexplained hypoglycaemia should prompt a review of insulin dose. Unexplained highs may be a "rebound" after hypoglycaemia (over-treatment plus counter-regulatory hormones). [Section 6.2, p. 1285]
- Day-to-day changes may be needed for exercise, diet, travel, school trips, camps and holidays. [Section 6.2, p. 1285]

## Injection technique and absorption

- Inject subcutaneously, not into muscle; intramuscular insulin is absorbed faster and unpredictably, more so during exercise. 4 mm pen needles carry the lowest risk of intramuscular injection; children ≤6 years and very thin people should use a lifted skinfold with 4 mm needles. [Section 7.1.1, Table 2, p. 1286–1287]
- Rotate within the same region and stay at least 1 cm from previous sites; avoid lipohypertrophy, which delays absorption. When moving injections from lipohypertrophy to healthy tissue, the dose often needs to drop by more than 20%. [Table 2, p. 1287; Section 7.1.3, p. 1288]
- Wait about 10–15 s (5–15 s after pushing the plunger) before withdrawing a pen needle; do a 2-unit air shot before each pen injection. [Section 7.1.1, p. 1286; Section 7.2.2, p. 1288]
- Glargine and detemir should not be mixed with other insulin; rapid analogues can be mixed with NPH just before injecting. [Section 7.1.1, p. 1287]
- Faster absorption: smaller doses and depots, local heat or hot surroundings (pump and MDI), exercise of the injected limb, intramuscular injection. Slower: lipohypertrophy, obesity. Regular insulin is absorbed fastest from the abdomen; site differences are smaller with analogues, and glargine and degludec are barely affected by site. [Section 7.1.3, p. 1288]

## Insulin storage

- Insulin degrades faster with heat, direct sunlight and shaking. Home fridges can drop below freezing. [Section 7.3.1, p. 1289]
- Not in use: refrigerate at 2–8 °C, away from the freezer section, until expiry. Throw away insulin that has been frozen or shows clumping, frosting, discoloration or precipitate. [Section 7.3.1, p. 1289]
- In use: room temperature (below 25 or 30 °C) for up to 4 weeks; vials are usable for 10 days to 8 weeks after opening depending on product — follow the label. [Section 7.3.2, p. 1289]
- Pumps: insulin in the reservoir should be changed more often. Manufacturers allow aspart up to 6 days and lispro up to 7 days in a reservoir at room temperature; ideally change insulin with the set every 48–72 h. Glulisine labelling allows 2 days at 37 °C. [Section 7.3.2, p. 1289]
- If insulin needs rise unexpectedly, consider lost potency. Do not leave pumps or insulin in the sun or in a car. [Section 7.3.1–7.3.2, p. 1289]
- Travel: carry insulin in hand luggage, never in checked bags; never place it directly on ice; take spare insulin. [Section 7.3.3, p. 1290]
- Every young person should have rapid-acting or regular insulin available for high glucose and ketosis emergencies, plus a small spare supply (E). [Executive summary, p. 1278]

## Diluted and concentrated insulin

Very young children may use diluted insulin for doses below 1 unit. Rapid-acting insulin can be diluted to U10 or U50 with the manufacturer's diluent and stored for 1 month for pump use; U20 has been used in AID in 3–6-year-olds. Dosing errors with unusual concentrations can be serious, so families must be trained and tell any new clinician which concentration they use. [Section 4.7, p. 1282–1283]

## Hospital and subcutaneous DKA treatment (brief)

IV insulin is the standard for DKA and critically ill children. Where IV infusion or analogues are not available, subcutaneous regular insulin every 4 h has been suggested for mild DKA, starting at about 0.13–0.17 U/kg per dose (0.8–1 U/kg/day), adjusted by 10–20% according to glucose before the next dose, and given every 2–3 h if acidosis is not improving. [Section 8, p. 1290]

## Limits of this summary

- The guideline is written for children and adolescents with type 1 diabetes; adult use is outside its scope, and most numbers are starting points, not individual settings.
- Many statements are expert consensus (grade E) or lower-grade evidence; the 500/1800/100 rules are rules of thumb that must be checked against real glucose data.
- Pump and AID details are in the separate ISPAD 2022 insulin delivery chapter; injection devices, affordability, biosimilars and research sections are only touched on here.
- Read the original and follow the care team's advice for any clinical decision.
