*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Gregory JW, Cameron FJ, Joshi K, et al. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Diabetes in adolescence. Pediatr Diabetes. 2022;23(7):857-871, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13408.*

## Key points

- Puberty brings physiological insulin resistance, stronger in T1D; glycaemic control often worsens in puberty and stays worse through adolescence [grade B] [Executive summary; Endocrinology of puberty].
- Insulin sensitivity in adolescents with T1D is about 33–42% lower than in peers without diabetes; higher growth hormone also drives stronger ketogenesis, which may predispose to decompensation and **DKA** [Endocrinology of puberty].
- Hypoglycaemia is the main factor that raises driving risk; with good hypo awareness, stable glucose and normal vision most young people can drive private vehicles [Driving].
- Adolescents with T1D are not more likely than peers to try alcohol or drugs, but knowing the risks does not mean they follow safe-drinking advice [Drug and alcohol use].
- DKA episodes and persistently very high HbA1c are red flags for mental health problems [grade B]; insulin omission or overdose seen on **CGM** should also prompt assessment [Mental health disorders].
- Transition to adult care should be planned [grade E]; care coordinators / patient navigators improve attendance after transfer [grade A] [Becoming a young adult].

## Puberty and insulin needs

Insulin needs rise in puberty because of growth hormone/IGF-1 changes, sex hormones and more lean body mass, while behaviour changes (erratic meals and exercise, less consistent insulin use, less physical activity) add to it. With modern insulin, puberty is normal or only slightly delayed [grade B]. Weight gain, particularly in girls, can affect body image and lead to disordered eating and insulin omission [Endocrinology of puberty].

## Alcohol and other substances

- Adolescents with T1D experiment with alcohol and substances at similar rates to peers; young adult men with T1D more often have a substance-use diagnosis, which may begin in the teens.
- Cigarettes, alcohol or drugs may be used to cope with diabetes distress.
- Awareness of alcohol risks does not reliably lead to safe drinking behaviour [Drug and alcohol use].
- This chapter gives no specific alcohol rules for glucose or insulin (see the ISPAD hypoglycaemia chapter for alcohol's effect on lows).

## Driving

- Regulations differ by country; severe hypoglycaemia in recent months may delay or suspend a licence in some places.
- Counselling should cover the local rules and preventing lows: check glucose before driving, use CGM, and eat appropriately [Driving].

## Hypoglycaemia in daily life, exams and work

- Discuss how hypo- and hyperglycaemia affect thinking before major exams. Exam accommodations should allow food and drink (to treat lows), toilet access and diabetes devices (meter, CGM, insulin delivery); insulin or diet may be adjusted to keep glucose in range [Study and examinations].
- Panic attacks overlap strongly with hypoglycaemia symptoms, which complicates both [Anxiety disorders].
- Young adults should tell peers and colleagues about hypo risks, symptoms and treatment as they become independent [Young adulthood and leaving home].
- Some careers may be restricted (e.g. police, firefighting, armed forces, large goods vehicles, piloting; rules vary) [Employment].

## Technology and peer support

- Despite new technology, good control in adolescence remains hard [What is new].
- Online diabetes communities and peer groups are an important source of support and of information about treatment and new technology; information quality there was rated generally high in a review. Clinicians should point young people to them and ask about them [grade B] [Communicating within the diabetes community].
- CGM data can reveal insulin omission or overdose [Mental health disorders].

## Mental health, briefly

About 30% of adolescents with T1D have diabetes distress, and questionnaire studies suggest about 30% have depression and about 30% significant anxiety symptoms; distress can be mistaken for depression. CBT is first-line; SSRIs have the best evidence where medication is needed. Eating disorders (1.6–21% depending on method) and insulin omission need joint care with eating-disorder services. After DKA, re-teach sick-day rules and assess mental health before discharge, especially with recurrent DKA [Mental health disorders].

## Transition to adult care

- Start preparation in early adolescence, at least 1 year before transfer; agree timing with the young person and family; delaying transfer into the early twenties may suit many.
- Provide a written summary and transition plan, direct handover between teams, and navigators to keep appointments; continue diabetes visits at least every 3 months.
- Young adults need education on self-management, keeping supplies and appointments, and **sick-day management** [Transition from pediatric to adult care].

## Other topics (not summarised in detail)

Sexual health (preconception counselling from early puberty; long-acting reversible contraception as first choice), school and family communication, authoritative parenting, and screening for social needs at least yearly [grade C] are covered in the original.

## Limits of this summary

- Population: adolescents and young adults with diabetes; most statements are grade B, C or E.
- The chapter gives little practical guidance on alcohol, hypoglycaemia treatment or technology settings; those are in other ISPAD chapters.
- Sexual health, school and social-determinant sections are left out or very brief.
- Read the original and follow the care team for clinical decisions.
