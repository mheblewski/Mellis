*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Biester T, Berget C, Boughton C, et al. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2024: Diabetes Technologies – Insulin Delivery. Horm Res Paediatr 2024;97(6):636–662, https://doi.org/10.1159/000543034.*

## Key points

- Offer youth the most advanced insulin delivery technology that is available, accessible and acceptable for them, and choose the system by individual needs and preferences (both grade A). [Introduction]
- Pumps are recommended regardless of age (A), baseline glycaemia (A) and diabetes duration (B). Infusion set failures can happen with any pump and must be caught early to prevent DKA (B). [Insulin Pumps, Recommendations]
- AID is strongly recommended for youth (A): it improves time in range (TIR) by lowering both hypo- and hyperglycaemia, improves person-reported outcomes and reduces burden, especially overnight. [Automated Insulin Delivery]
- New in 2024: separate AID recommendations for newly diagnosed children (A), preschool children (A), school-aged children (A), adolescents (A) and young adults (A). [AID sections]
- Trials show about 10–15 percentage points more TIR (3.9–10 mmol/L, 70–180 mg/dL) and 0.3–0.7% lower HbA1c with AID; time in automation above 70% goes with reaching TIR ≥70%. [Data from Large Clinical Trials; Young Adults]
- Carbohydrate counting or a record of consistent bolusing should not be a precondition for AID; even inconsistent bolusers benefit, and fixed meal doses can improve TIR. [Practical Considerations for Behavioral, Psychosocial, and Educational Considerations]
- Hypoglycaemia treatment may need fewer carbohydrates after prolonged basal suspension on integrated systems; sick-day and ketone training is still needed because insulin is delivered the same way as on any pump. [Practical Considerations for AID]
- If people choose open-source AID, support from care providers is encouraged (E). [Non-Certified Open-Source AID Approaches]

## What changed versus ISPAD 2022

- More detail on AID, combining trial data with real-world evidence, and age-specific evidence from preschoolers to young adults. [Summary of What Is New or Different]
- The pump recommendation now explicitly includes "regardless of baseline glycaemia and duration". [Insulin Pumps, Recommendations]
- The earlier DKA warning (2–5-fold higher risk on pumps) is softened: recent studies show this excess risk has become smaller. [The Evidence for Insulin Pump Therapy]
- New statements that AID suits children from diagnosis and through the honeymoon phase, and that carb counting is not a prerequisite. [AID Systems in Newly Diagnosed Children; Practical Considerations]
- Benefits beyond glucose (quality of life, sleep, burden, satisfaction) get their own grade B recommendation; training may be in person or remote. [Behavioral, Psychosocial, and Educational Considerations]
- The 2022 table of starting pump rules (TDD, 500/1800 rules) moved to an online supplement (eTable 1) not included in our copy. [Initiating Pump Therapy]
- Several non-AID recommendations are now framed for people without AID access: PLGS (A), then LGS (A), and SAP with ≥60% sensor wear (A). [Non-AID]

## Pump therapy: bolus calculator, insulin on board, extended and combo bolus

- Pump bolus calculators use the carbohydrate entry, current glucose and insulin on board, which helps prevent insulin stacking. [Initiating Pump Therapy]
- Initial settings are derived from the total daily dose. DPV registry data: children under 6 needed more basal from 6 pm to midnight; adolescents (12–18) and young adults (18–25) needed more basal around 3–8 am. [Initiating Pump Therapy]
- Better HbA1c is associated with more pre-programmed basal rates, more daily boluses and basal under 50% of the total daily dose. Meal boluses and announcements should be stressed at every visit. [Initiating Pump Therapy]
- Temporary basal rates lower delivery before, during and after physical activity, or raise it for illness; reduction or full suspension can help prevent exercise hypoglycaemia. Separate basal patterns can be used for times like menstruation. [Advanced Pump Features]
- Bolus shapes: standard, extended (square), or combo (dual wave). High-fat meals may be best handled with extended or combo boluses because fat delays the rise; example split 40% now and 60% over 4 hours. Reducing the bolus by active insulin may lower post-bolus and severe hypoglycaemia. [Advanced Pump Features]
- Diluted insulin can deliver very small doses in very young children, but not all systems are approved for it. [Initiating Pump Therapy]

## Infusion sets, DKA and complications

- 40–68% of pump users have pump-related adverse events (set failures, malfunctions, alarm problems). No set type is proven best; steel cannulas kink less and may suit the youngest. Occlusion or dislodgement is the key DKA risk. Fault-detection algorithms using sensor and delivery data, and continuous ketone monitors, are being explored. [Complications of Insulin Pump Therapy; The Evidence for Insulin Pump Therapy]
- Education on DKA risk and on handling persistent hyperglycaemia remains essential. Users must know when to check ketones, change the infusion site and give insulin by injection; this applies equally to AID. [The Evidence for Insulin Pump Therapy; Education and Training]
- Rotate the infusion site with each new insertion to avoid lipohypertrophy; affected areas can take months to heal. Local eczema at cannula sites was seen in 14% of youth in one study. [Complications of Insulin Pump Therapy]

## AID: how systems work and their limits

- AID adjusts insulin to sensor glucose, unlike LGS and predictive low glucose management, which only suspend insulin. Components: pump, CGM and algorithm; no single best algorithm has emerged. [Automated Insulin Delivery]
- Fully automated systems without meal announcement caused large post-meal rises, so hybrid systems require the user to bolus for carbohydrates. [Automated Insulin Delivery]
- Targets are set as a single value (for example 5.8 mmol/L / 105 mg/dL) or a range (for example 6.2–8.9 mmol/L / 112–160 mg/dL); system labels may also require a minimum insulin dose, age or weight. [Automated Insulin Delivery]
- Medtronic 780G example (FLAIR): selectable targets 100/110/120 mg/dL (5.6/6.1/6.7 mmol/L), automatic correction boluses when glucose is above 120 mg/dL (6.7 mmol/L) and maximum automated basal is reached, and meal detection that allows stronger auto-corrections. TIR went from 57% at baseline to 63% (670G) and 67% (780G); time in automation was 75% vs 86%. More targeted results came with the lowest target (100 mg/dL; 5.6 mmol/L). [Adolescents; School-Aged Children]
- In one study auto-corrections made up about 70% of all bolus insulin. Young adults show the fewest user-initiated boluses and the most automatic corrections, yet TIR benefit is similar across ages. [Adolescents; Young Adults]
- iLet (Beta Bionics) uses qualitative meal announcements instead of carbohydrate grams and is initialised from body weight only. [School-Aged Children]
- Reported safety events in trials included DKA from catheter or infusion-site failure and hypoglycaemia after a bolus when the meal was delayed, and severe lows after manual boluses. [School-Aged Children; Adolescents]
- Exercise: users should be guided on managing it (ISPAD exercise chapter); the 2024 chapter gives no new exercise numbers. [Practical Considerations for AID]

## AID by age group

- **Newly diagnosed (A):** two RCTs with up to 4 years of follow-up showed higher TIR and less time above range from diagnosis, with differences emerging 6–9 months in; AID was safe through the honeymoon period; no beta-cell preservation effect; outcomes were similar whether or not the child had DKA at diagnosis. [AID Systems in Newly Diagnosed Children]
- **Preschool (A):** CamAPS FX (1–7 years), Omnipod 5 (2–6 years, +10.9 points TIR, gains kept for 2 years), MiniMed 670G/780G and Control-IQ (2–6 years, TIR 56.7% → 69.3%) all improved TIR without more time below range. Loop (open-source) in 67 children under 7: TIR 67% → 73% over 6 months. Real-world Omnipod 5 at a 110 mg/dL target: 68.8% met <4% time below range and 57% met >70% TIR. Parents valued remote access to insulin data as well as glucose. [Preschool Children]
- **School age (A):** Control-IQ from 6 years and Medtronic from 7 years; trials of different systems show roughly 8–16 points more TIR without more time below range. [School-Aged Children]
- **Adolescents (A) and young adults (A):** the most difficult age for glucose; AID still adds about 10–14 points TIR; those with the highest HbA1c or lowest TIR at start gain most. [Adolescents; Young Adults]

## Non-AID options

- PLGS is strongly recommended for people with T1D without access to AID (A); Basal-IQ cut time <3.9 mmol/L (<70 mg/dL) by 31%; PLGS overnight lowered hypoglycaemia risk by 8.8%. [Predictive LGS Systems]
- LGS is recommended when AID and PLGS are unavailable (A). [LGS Systems]
- SAP is recommended over MDI if sensors are worn at least 60% of the time (A); each 10% more wear adds about 1.1 points TIR. [Sensor-Augmented Pump]
- Connected pens may be offered to youth who prefer not to wear a device (C); they track insulin on board and help prevent stacking. [Connected Insulin Pens]

## Open-source AID

Several thousand people use DIY AID. Evidence cited: simulations of mis-estimated and late meal boluses, a real-world study of 558 users with better TIR and fewer severe lows, and an RCT (ages 7–70) with about 14% adjusted TIR difference versus SAP — though that trial used a preset device with clinical support, unlike typical DIY use. Clinicians should be cautious about preferring unapproved systems over approved ones but are encouraged to support people who choose them (E) and to learn the systems' key features so they can help optimise settings safely. [Non-Certified Open-Source AID Approaches]

## Education, expectations and quality of life

- AID is recommended to reduce burden, improve perceived sleep and raise treatment satisfaction (B). [Behavioral, Psychosocial, and Educational Considerations]
- Counsel realistic expectations about glucose results and the effort needed (C). Use structured training with follow-up in the first few weeks; remote training works about as well as in-person (C). [Behavioral, Psychosocial, and Educational Considerations; Education and Training]
- Most stop devices in the first 1–3 months, so review use and adjust settings such as carbohydrate ratios within the first month. [Education and Training]
- Remaining barriers: workload, technical glitches and exits from automation, alarms at night, device visibility. These were more common with first-generation systems; factory-calibrated CGM reduces burden. [Behavioral and Psychosocial Outcomes]

## Limits of this summary

- Written for children, adolescents and young adults with type 1 diabetes; device-specific numbers come from individual trials and differ by system and settings.
- Most AID recommendations are grade A; open-source support is expert opinion (E); connected pens and education points are grade C.
- Online supplements (starting pump settings, CARES framework, real-world tables) were not in our copy and are not covered.
- Read the original and follow the care team's advice for any clinical decision.
