*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Annan SF, Higgins LA, Jelleryd E, et al. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Nutritional management in children and adolescents with diabetes. Pediatric Diabetes 2022, doi 10.1111/pedi.13429, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13429.*

Page numbers below refer to the 25-page early-view PDF of the chapter. Letters A, B, C, E after a point are the ISPAD evidence grades given in the chapter.

## Key points

- Matching the meal insulin dose to the carbohydrate eaten (intensive regimens, individual insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio, ICR) gives more freedom with meals and better glycaemia and quality of life (A). An individual meal plan with prandial insulin adjustment improves glycaemic outcomes (A). [Executive summary, p. 1]
- Meal insulin should be given before eating, from diagnosis onwards, at every age (A). A bolus 15–20 min before the meal gives a lower post-meal rise than one given just before eating; newer rapid-acting insulins also need pre-meal dosing. [p. 2; Section 10.2, p. 12]
- Fat and protein change both the early and the delayed glucose rise after a meal (A); meals higher in fat and protein need a change in both the insulin amount and the way it is delivered (A). [p. 2; Section 10.1, p. 11]
- High-fat or high-protein meals lower the early (1–2 h) rise and raise glucose later, up to about 3–6 h after eating. [Section 10.1, p. 11]
- Suggested starting point for extra insulin for fat and protein: about 20% more than the dose calculated for carbohydrate alone, used only when the person's own post-meal data show the effect. [Section 10.1.2, p. 11]
- On pumps, a combination (dual-wave / split) bolus works better than a standard bolus for high-fat, high-protein meals; studied splits range from 60/40 or 70/30 over 3 h (children) to a mean of 30/70 over about 2.4 h (adults). [Section 10.2, p. 12]
- Glycaemic index (GI) adds benefit on top of carbohydrate counting (B) but should not be used on its own. Cut-offs: high GI ≥70, medium 56–69, low ≤55. [p. 2; Section 9, p. 10]
- No method of counting carbohydrate (grams, 10–12 g portions, 15 g exchanges) has been shown to be better than another (E). [p. 2; Section 8.4, p. 10]
- Guide to daily energy split: carbohydrate about 40–50%, fat under 35% (saturated fat under 10%), protein 15–25% (C). [p. 1; Box 1, p. 3]
- Alcohol raises the risk of hypoglycaemia, including delayed hypoglycaemia 8–12 h after drinking. [Section 5.7, p. 6]
- For sport: carbohydrate 30–60 g per hour during exercise longer than 60 min, 1–1.5 g/kg within 30 min after the session, protein 20 g shortly after exercise. [Box 5, p. 14]

## Carbohydrate counting and the insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio (ICR)

- Carbohydrate counting is best started at diagnosis for anyone on intensive insulin therapy (MDI or pump), together with teaching how mixed meals affect glucose after eating (E). [p. 2; Section 8.2, p. 9]
- The amount of carbohydrate and the pre-meal bolus are among the strongest drivers of post-meal glucose; GI, fat, protein and fibre also matter and should be considered when reading post-meal curves. [Section 8.2, p. 9]
- The ICR is set per child (age, sex, puberty, diabetes duration, activity). Younger children on pumps often use a lower share of basal insulin and so need relatively "stronger" ratios (more insulin per gram). Adult-style formulas such as the 500 rule can give ratios that are too weak in children; the chapter mentions 250 or 330 rules for younger children. Breakfast may need a stronger ratio than other meals. [Section 8.4, p. 9]
- Reading the post-meal curve: in the first hour the rise mostly reflects insulin timing; from about 90 min to 2 h, the amount and GI of the carbohydrate; later, meal composition (fat, protein). [Section 8.4, p. 9–10]
- Bolus calculators for MDI and pumps help with dose calculation, may improve post-meal glucose and reduce fear of hypoglycaemia. [Section 8.4, p. 10]
- Accuracy and consistency of counting matter for post-meal glucose and variability. Children and parents can count reasonably well, but over- and under-estimation are common, so skills need regular review as children grow and new foods appear. Simplified methods (for example hand-size portions) are an option where numeracy or literacy is limited. [Section 8.4, p. 10]
- Fixed (twice-daily) insulin regimens need consistent carbohydrate amounts and timing, usually including a bedtime snack to prevent nocturnal hypoglycaemia; the chapter says these regimens should not be used when other options exist (C). [p. 1; Section 8.3.1, p. 9]

## Fat and protein: insulin dosing for mixed meals (FPU, extra insulin %)

Why it matters [Section 10.1, p. 11]:
- Meals high in protein or fat reduce the early rise (1–2 h) and increase delayed hyperglycaemia (around 3–6 h). Carbohydrate-only dosing formulas therefore have limits.

Methods that have been tried [Section 10.1, p. 11]:
- **Fat-protein units (FPU)**: improved post-meal time in range in a paediatric comparison but with more hypoglycaemia; several studies saw more clinically significant hypoglycaemia with the original FPU formula. A modified approach has been suggested in which 200 kcal from protein is treated as needing the same insulin as 10 g of carbohydrate, which may reduce hypoglycaemia.
- **Food Insulin Index (FII)**: mixed results in adults; no benefit over carbohydrate counting in a paediatric comparison.
- **Percentage increase on top of the carbohydrate dose**: described as the more practical option.

Pumps (CSII) [Section 10.1.1, p. 11]:
- Reviews give a range of suggestions: step-wise increases up to about 30–35% for high-fat, high-protein meals with an extended bolus; other reviews put the extra need at 25–75%, with a starting point of up to 60% more insulin given 15 min before a high-protein, high-fat meal as a combination bolus, the remainder spread over 3 h.
- Individual needs differ a lot, so advice should be based on the person's own glucose for up to 6 h after the meal.

Injections (MDI) [Section 10.1.2, p. 11]:
- Extra insulin for high-fat, high-protein meals can be given in the pre-meal injection. One study used 125% of the carbohydrate dose before a high-fat, high-protein breakfast without adverse effects. Another, which counted carbohydrate, fat and protein, improved post-meal glucose without more hypoglycaemia.
- One adult study of a high-fat, high-carbohydrate meal gave 130% of the meal dose as a split (100% before the meal, 30% about 3 h later); this matched the low-fat control meal without more hypoglycaemia, whereas giving the same 130% at once increased hypoglycaemia. [Section 10.2, p. 12]
- Some centres use regular (soluble) insulin for a longer effect, but two studies found no advantage over a rapid analogue (aspart). [Section 10.2, p. 12]

Practical recommendations [Section 10.1.2, p. 11]:
- Adjust for fat and protein only when the individual's data show a post-meal effect; a suggested start is +20% on the carbohydrate dose.
- Teach from diagnosis how to read the curve: high glucose in the first 60–90 min points to insulin timing, at 90–120 min to the carbohydrate amount, and from about 120 to 300+ min to fat, protein and meal composition.
- Food choices can be used deliberately: adding protein to breakfast to blunt the spike, or choosing higher-protein meals when delayed hypoglycaemia is a risk. Adding a moderate amount of protein to a mostly-carbohydrate meal can reduce the post-meal rise. [Section 5.1.1, p. 4]
- Glucose checks before and at 1, 3, 5 and 7 h after the meal, or CGM, help to guide and evaluate dose and timing changes. [Section 10.2, p. 12]

Hybrid closed loop (HCL / AID) [Section 10.1.2, p. 11; Section 10.2, p. 12]:
- Fat and protein handling on HCL systems is not yet well studied in children or adults. Clinical experience suggests some people still need individual strategies. The dietitian needs to understand how the algorithm adjusts insulin and which bolus options exist. Bolus timing remains important on HCL, and the ICR stays one of the settings the user can change.

## Split, extended and combination bolus; bolus timing

- Pre-meal bolus is better than a bolus during or after the meal; 15–20 min before eating improves post-meal glucose compared with dosing just before. Missed meal boluses worsen glycaemic outcomes. [Section 10.2, p. 12]
- Up to 15–20 min early dosing is also listed as a way to reduce post-meal peaks, whatever the carbohydrate content of the diet. [Section 5.1.1, p. 4]
- Pumps allow the bolus shape to match the meal (low GI, high fat, high protein). A systematic review could not define one duration and split for all meals; the best pattern also varies within the same person. [Section 10.2, p. 12]
- Studied patterns for high-fat, high-protein meals: in children and adolescents, 60/40 or 70/30 over 3 h; in adults, a mean of 30/70 over about 2.4 h, individual range 10/90 to 50/50 over 2–3 h. In practice the combination bolus needs enough insulin up front to handle the first rise. [Section 10.2, p. 12]
- High-GI foods: earlier insulin may blunt the spike. Lower-GI foods: a combination bolus may help. [Section 9, p. 11]
- Toddlers with erratic eating: a pre-meal dose is preferred, but the dose may be split, part before and the rest during the meal, when intake is unpredictable or new foods are offered. Grazing should be discouraged. [Section 11.1, p. 12]

## Glycaemic index (GI) and glycaemic load (GL)

- GI is helpful on top of carbohydrate quantity (B), never instead of it. Categories: high ≥70, medium 56–69, low ≤55. [Section 9, p. 10]
- GI is changed by cooking and preparation, physical form, type of starch and the fat and protein eaten with the food. Low-GI foods (wholegrain bread, pasta, temperate fruit, dairy) can lower the post-meal rise when they replace high-GI foods. Dairy, legumes, pasta and fruit are fairly consistent worldwide; cereals, breads, rice and potatoes vary widely. [Section 9, p. 10]
- CGM or scanned glucose data can show individual responses to specific foods. [Section 9, p. 11]
- Glycaemic load (GI × amount) has only small pilot data for dosing; more research is needed. [Section 9, p. 11]

## Macronutrients, sugar, fibre and special diets

- Daily guide (Box 1): carbohydrate 40–50% of energy, sucrose up to 10%, fat 30–40% with saturated plus trans fat under 10%, protein 15–25%. Overweight older adolescents may use lower carbohydrate (around 40%) with higher protein (around 25%). Where food is scarce, carbohydrate may need to be up to 60% of energy. [Section 4.3, Box 1, p. 3–4]
- Sucrose does not raise glucose more than the same energy from starch, but sugary drinks give peaks that are hard to cover with insulin and should be discouraged; diet drinks are an alternative. Sucrose may be used instead of glucose to prevent or treat hypoglycaemia (details are in the ISPAD hypoglycaemia chapter, not in this one). [Section 5.1.2, p. 5]
- Low-carbohydrate (<26% energy) and very-low-carbohydrate (20–50 g/day) diets: evidence in young people with T1D is lacking. Risks named: ketosis, dyslipidaemia, disordered eating, nutritional gaps and poor growth, more hypoglycaemia, and possibly a weaker effect of glucagon in severe hypoglycaemia. Families choosing them should work with a dietitian. [Section 5.1.1, p. 4]
- Protein needs fall from about 2 g/kg/day in infancy to about 1 g/kg/day at 10 years and 0.8–0.9 g/kg/day in late adolescence; supplements are usually unnecessary. With kidney disease, avoid intake above 25% of energy. [Section 5.4, p. 6]
- Non-nutritive sweeteners are safe within acceptable daily intakes (Box 4); polyols can cause diarrhoea from about 20 g, especially in children. Special "diabetic" foods are not needed. [Section 5.8, Box 4, p. 7]
- Coeliac disease: gluten-free diet; gluten-free eating can increase glucose swings, and lower-GI, higher-fibre choices with early pre-meal insulin may help. Uncontaminated oats may be tolerated in moderate amounts (about 20–25 g/day dry oats for children). [Section 15.2, p. 16]

## Alcohol and substance use

[Section 5.7, p. 6–7]
- Alcohol suppresses gluconeogenesis and dulls hypoglycaemia awareness; delayed hypoglycaemia can occur 8–12 h after drinking. Sugary drinks may first raise glucose, with the alcohol causing a low later. Extra carbohydrate eaten to avoid lows can cause highs.
- Advice to give: avoid binge drinking (more than four standard drinks); alternate alcoholic drinks with sugar-free non-alcoholic ones; be cautious with "diabetic" or low-carbohydrate beers, which often are not lower in alcohol; carry ID and fast-acting carbohydrate.
- To prevent night-time lows: a carbohydrate snack at bedtime, more frequent checks overnight and the next day at least until lunchtime; CGM helps.
- Heavy drinking can cause vomiting and dehydration leading to DKA. Cannabis is linked to irregular monitoring and dosing and, in adults, to a higher DKA risk.

## Hypoglycaemia and weight

- Regular review of insulin doses as children grow reduces the need for large snacks to prevent lows. For physical activity, adjusting insulin is preferred over eating extra carbohydrate to prevent hypoglycaemia. [Section 4.2, p. 3]
- CGM can help judge how much carbohydrate is needed to treat a low and avoid over-treatment, which adds to weight gain. [Section 4.2, p. 3]
- This chapter does not give gram amounts for treating hypoglycaemia; it refers to the ISPAD 2022 hypoglycaemia chapter. [Section 5.1.2, p. 5]

## Exercise and sport nutrition

[Section 13, Box 5, p. 13–15]
- General fitness does not need extra energy above normal needs; training more than 2 h a day does.
- Box 5 guide: protein 1.2–1.8 g/kg/day with 20 g soon after exercise; carbohydrate about 50% of energy or 3–8 g/kg/day depending on intensity; 30–60 g/h during exercise lasting over 60 min; 1–1.5 g/kg within 30 min after finishing; fat no more than 30% of energy. Fluid: 5–7 ml/kg 4 h before; during exercise enough to limit body-mass loss to under 2%; afterwards 460–675 ml per 0.5 kg lost.
- Before exercise (1–3 h): a low-fat meal containing carbohydrate. Hypoglycaemia risk is higher when exercise falls at peak insulin action. Some high-intensity or anaerobic activities may need extra bolus insulin for pre-exercise carbohydrate, and food before competition may need more insulin than before training.
- During exercise: aerobic exercise of 60 min or more may need extra carbohydrate spread over the session; isotonic drinks with 6–8% carbohydrate suit sessions over 1 h. Practise fuelling in training.
- After exercise: replace glycogen and prevent post-exercise lows caused by higher insulin sensitivity; carbohydrate with protein may help prevent late lows; needs may be up to 1.5 g/kg; post-exercise carbohydrate needs carefully adjusted insulin. A milk drink after exercise reduced nocturnal hypoglycaemia compared with carbohydrate-only drinks in one study. Protein in the pre-exercise meal may lower hypoglycaemia risk during exercise.
- Low energy availability (RED-S), common in some female and adolescent athletes, likely raises hypoglycaemia risk. Caffeine may help prevent hypoglycaemia. Supplements are mostly unnecessary.
- Detailed insulin adjustments for exercise are in the ISPAD 2022 exercise chapter.

## Special situations: festivities, fasting, sick days

- Feasts often bring high-GI, high-fat, salty meals; counting carbohydrate, protein and fat, with extra insulin and a suitable bolus type, can help with the delayed rises. CGM helps during fasting and feasting. Fasting is covered in the ISPAD Ramadan chapter. [Section 12, p. 13]
- Sick-day nutrition (fluids, carbohydrate during illness, ketones) is not covered in this chapter; see the ISPAD 2022 sick-day chapter. Only vomiting and dehydration from alcohol are named here as a DKA risk. [Section 5.7, p. 7]

## Age groups and disordered eating

- Toddlers: routine small meals, no grazing, no return to bottles of milk or juice for "easy" carbohydrate; carers need training. [Section 11.1, p. 12]
- School: regular meal and snack plan, time for checks and treatment, supervised pre-meal insulin. [Section 11.1.1, p. 12]
- Adolescents: irregular routines, skipped insulin and meals, alcohol; weight loss or failure to gain weight may point to insulin omission and disordered eating. [Section 11.2, p. 13]
- Repeated DKA or worsening glycaemia may be a sign of disordered eating (C). Insulin restriction for weight control is more common in girls and young women; the DEPS-R questionnaire is a validated screening tool. [p. 2; Section 15.3, p. 17]

## Limits of this summary

- The chapter is written for children and adolescents (and refers young adults 18–24 years to adult nutrition guidance); many dosing data for fat and protein and for split boluses come from small studies, often in adults.
- Fat/protein percentages and bolus splits are starting points from a range of studies with large individual differences; the chapter says to individualise them using post-meal glucose up to 6 h. Evidence for HCL/AID systems is limited.
- Left out or only briefly covered: vitamins and minerals, sodium, fibre targets, food security, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia and the full eating-disorder section. Hypoglycaemia treatment amounts and sick-day management are not in this chapter.
- Read the original chapter (and the ISPAD hypoglycaemia, sick-day and exercise chapters) before making clinical decisions.
