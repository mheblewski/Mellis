# Increasing the protein quantity in a meal results in dose-dependent effects on postprandial glucose levels in individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus

- **Authors:** Paterson MA, Smart CEM, Lopez PE, Howley P, McElduff P, Attia J, Morbey C, King BR
- **Journal/year:** Diabetic Medicine 2017;34(6):851-854
- **DOI:** 10.1111/dme.13347
- **PubMed:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28257160/
- **Licence:** not open access (or open-access status not confirmed). This file contains a paraphrased summary of the abstract and key numbers written for the Mellis corpus, not the publisher's text.

## Summary of the abstract (paraphrased)

- **Population:** 27 people with T1D aged 10-40 years (HbA1c ≤ 64 mmol/mol [8%]), pump or MDI.
- **Design:** 30 g carbohydrate drink (negligible fat) with 0, 12.5, 25, 50 or 75 g whey protein added, randomised order over 5 days; a standard insulin dose for the carbohydrate only; 5 h CGM.
- **Early effect (0-60 min):** more protein gave significantly *lower* glucose excursions at 30 and 60 min (P = 0.007 and P = 0.002). No relationship at 90-120 min.
- **Late effect (150-300 min):** the relationship reversed: more protein gave *higher* glucose at every time point from 150 to 300 min (P < 0.004). All protein-added drinks gave significantly higher excursions than control from 150 to 300 min (P < 0.005).
- **Magnitude:** with 75 g protein, the mean excursion was **about 5 mmol/L (≈ 90 mg/dL) higher** than control (P < 0.001).

## Notes for Mellis

- Compare with Paterson 2016 (articles/PMC5064639.md): protein *alone* needed ≥ 75 g to raise glucose, but protein *with carbohydrate* raised late glucose at every amount tested from 12.5 g upward. This is why thresholds for "high protein" differ depending on whether carbohydrate is in the meal.
- Both effects (early lowering, late rise) argue against giving all extra insulin upfront.
