*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Paterson MA, Smart CE, Lopez PE, McElduff P, Attia J, Morbey C, King BR. Influence of dietary protein on postprandial blood glucose levels in individuals with Type 1 diabetes mellitus using intensive insulin therapy. Diabetic Medicine 2016;33(5):592-598, https://doi.org/10.1111/dme.13011.*

## Key points

- 27 people with type 1 diabetes aged 7-40 drank whey protein (0, 12.5, 25, 50, 75 or 100 g) or glucose (10 or 20 g) with no insulin, 4 hours after a standard evening meal, and were followed for 5 hours on CGM [Abstract; Methods].
- Protein alone up to 50 g did not cause a significant glucose rise over 5 hours compared with water [Abstract; Results].
- 75 g and 100 g protein lowered glucose slightly at 60-120 minutes, then raised it from 180 to 300 minutes: about +0.7 and +1.1 mmol/L at 180-240 min, and +1.65 and +1.72 mmol/L (about 30 mg/dL) at 240-300 min versus water [Table 2].
- The late rise from 75-100 g protein reached a level similar to 20 g glucose without insulin, but started only at about 90-100 minutes, came near that level around 180 minutes and was still rising at 5 hours [Mean time to peak glucose rise; Discussion].
- The authors conclude that 75 g or more of protein eaten alone affects glucose 3-5 hours later and support considering extra insulin for large protein loads, ideally as an extended (dual-wave) bolus for pump users [Conclusions; Discussion].

## Design

- Randomized crossover (8×8 Latin square), two centres in Newcastle, Australia; pump (14) or MDI (12) users; mean age about 22, HbA1c 6.9% (52 mmol/mol), healthy BMI [Patients and methods; Table 1].
- Protein amounts were chosen from the Pankowska fat-protein unit (FPU) idea, in which 100 kcal of fat and/or protein (25 g protein) counts as one unit; they also reflect restaurant steak sizes (a 300 g steak has about 90 g protein) [Test drinks; Patients and methods].
- Days were repeated if starting glucose was above 13 or below 3.6 mmol/L (about 234 or 65 mg/dL) [Study procedure].

## Protein and glucose: the numbers

- 20 g glucose was the only drink with a clear rise at 60-120 minutes (+1.97 mmol/L versus water) [Table 2].
- 25 g protein gave lower glucose than water throughout; 12.5 g and 50 g did not differ from water [Table 2].
- The odds of glucose above 10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL) from 180-300 minutes were higher after several protein loads (OR about 1.9-2.7) and after 20 g glucose [Results].
- The results fit the older estimate that roughly 1 g of glucose appears for every 5 g of protein, so 75-100 g protein behaves late like about 15-20 g glucose [Discussion].
- When protein is part of a mixed meal, smaller amounts matter: an earlier study found 40 g added to a meal raised glucose, whereas isolated protein needed 75 g or more [Discussion].

## Clinical notes from the authors

- Protein-rich meals may help prevent night-time hypoglycaemia, and protein should be considered when judging basal rates; skipping a meal may test basal needs better than eating a carb-free meal [Discussion].
- Relevant to people on high-protein, low-carbohydrate diets [Discussion].
- Possible mechanisms are glucagon release and gluconeogenesis from amino acids; not settled [Discussion].

## Limits of this summary

- Mixed group of children, adolescents and young adults; isolated whey drinks, not real meals; no insulin was given for the drinks.
- Observation stopped at 5 hours although glucose was still rising; CGM was unblinded.
- The study does not say how much extra insulin to give or how to split it; read the original for clinical decisions.
