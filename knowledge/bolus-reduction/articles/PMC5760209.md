*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Laffel LM, Aleppo G, Buckingham BA, Forlenza GP, Rasbach LE, Tsalikian E, Weinzimer SA, Harris DR. A Practical Approach to Using Trend Arrows on the Dexcom G5 CGM System to Manage Children and Adolescents With Diabetes. J Endocr Soc 2017;1(12):1461–1476, https://doi.org/10.1210/js.2017-00389.*

## Key points

- For youth aged 2 years and older, the group adds or subtracts a fixed number of **units** to the standard dose (ICR for carbs + correction with the correction factor, CF), chosen by arrow and by one of five CF bands that roughly follow age [Section 5; Fig. 3].
- Use it only **before meals** and for corrections **3 or more hours after a meal**; avoid it right after meals and in children who graze [Fig. 3; Section 6].
- Falling arrows: deductions run from none (very sensitive toddlers, CF ≥125 mg/dL/U, with ↘ or ↓) up to −4.0 U (CF <25 with ↓↓) [Fig. 3].
- With ↓↓ before a meal, consider giving insulin closer to the meal; with ↑↑, consider 15–30 minutes before eating [Fig. 3].
- If a downward adjustment makes the dose negative, consider whether fast-acting carbohydrate is needed, taking current glucose and past or planned exercise into account [Section 5].
- Wait at least 3 hours after a meal bolus before another correction (assumes active insulin time ≥3 h on a pump) [Section 6].
- Bedtime goal about 130 mg/dL (7.2 mmol/L) with a flat or gently rising arrow; be cautious adding insulin at bedtime [Section 7A].
- Before or during exercise with glucose ≤125 mg/dL (≈6.9 mmol/L) and a down arrow, 8–30 g fast-acting carbohydrate may be taken, scaled to age, expected response and arrow [Section 7B].
- Expert opinion with no trial of the method; Dexcom educational grant [Introduction].

## Trend arrows and expected 30-minute change

Same Dexcom G5 definitions as the adult paper: ↘/↗ 1–2 mg/dL/min (30–60 mg/dL in 30 min), ↓/↑ 2–3 mg/dL/min (60–90 mg/dL), ↓↓/↑↑ >3 mg/dL/min (>90 mg/dL); flat = not more than 1 mg/dL/min; no arrow = rate not available. Arrows use about the last 10 minutes of data; forecasts beyond 20–30 minutes are less accurate [Fig. 1]. Conversion used by the paper: mg/dL × 0.0555 = mmol/L.

### Insulin sensitivity bands and age

[Section 5; Fig. 3 note] Younger children are usually more sensitive (higher CF); sensitivity falls with puberty, and outliers exist.
- CF ≥125: highly sensitive, often toddlers/preschoolers (about ages 2–6).
- CF 75–<125: young school-age children.
- CF 50–<75: older school-age children (ages 7–12 vary most).
- CF 25–<50: early pubertal teens.
- CF <25: pubertal teens; adolescents/young adults (13–22) often use 25–50 or <25.

The steps respect 0.5 U minimum pen increments, so the table works for MDI and pumps, and it does not require insulin on board to be set to 3 hours [Section 5; Fig. 3]. The authors note the table is more conservative than earlier methods for low CF (e.g., <30) and more aggressive in the mid-range (40–75), and conservative against a 60-minute horizon [Fig. 4].

## Bolus reduction for falling glucose before a meal: worked cases

[Table 4]
- 3-year-old, lunch, 275 mg/dL with ↓↓, target 150, 40 g, CF 125, ICR 1:20: calculated 3.0 U, minus 0.5 U → 2.5 U.
- 12-year-old, 2 h after soccer practice, 105 mg/dL with ↓↓, 75 g, CF 60, ICR 1:15: 5.0 U for carbs, minus 2.0 U → 3.0 U, to prevent a later low.
- 5-year-old, bedtime snack 15 g, 150 mg/dL with ↓, target 130, CF 120, ICR 1:30: 0.5 U, parents choose the −0.5 U option → 0 U to limit night-time hypo risk.
- Rising examples: 9-year-old 160 mg/dL ↑, CF 80 → +0.5 U (2.5 → 3.0 U); 16-year-old 75 mg/dL ↑↑, CF 25 → +3.0 U (9.0 → 12.0 U).
- Exercise cases: with recess after lunch (7-year-old) or after a hard hockey day at bedtime (17-year-old), the authors suggest skipping the upward adjustment.

## Safety: insulin stacking, missed boluses, grazing

[Sections 5–6]
- Avoid extra boluses for fast post-meal rises, especially with much insulin on board; post-meal rises are larger when insulin is given after eating, as in very young children.
- Missed or miscalculated boluses: do not use the arrow table; dose from the glucose at the meal and the ICR, preferably within 2 h. Also do not use it after over-treating a low.
- Grazing children: arrow adjustments can cause stacking and lows, so avoid.

## Bedtime, exercise, sick days, acetaminophen

- Bedtime: near-target glucose with down arrows may need an extra snack; above target with up arrows may get a modest correction; check overnight; consider changing alerts after unusual activity or illness [Section 7A].
- Exercise: children's activity is often unplanned; nocturnal lows are common after afternoon exercise; generally do not use the table during and after exercise; use arrows to decide on extra carbs (a single down arrow needs less than a double) [Section 7B].
- Sick days: fingerstick every 2–4 h, consider corrections every 2–3 h with ketone testing; use fingerstick for decisions when >250 mg/dL with ketones, or <70 mg/dL or symptoms [Section 7C].
- Acetaminophen (paracetamol) can give falsely high readings and distorted arrows for up to and beyond 6 h; base all treatment on fingersticks for 6 h. Do not rely solely on CGM in the first 24 h of a sensor [Sections 1, 7C].

## Limits of this summary

- Children and adolescents ≥2 years on MDI or open-loop pumps with Dexcom G5; automated insulin delivery (Trio/Loop) is not discussed.
- Expert opinion; no clinical trial has tested the table.
- Patient-selection lists, education checklist, data-sharing and the comparison figure are summarised briefly or left out; the comparison with DirecNet, Scheiner and Pettus/Edelman is the same as in the adult paper.
- Read the original and agree doses with the child's diabetes team before clinical use.
