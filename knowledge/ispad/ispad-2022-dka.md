*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Glaser N, Fritsch M, Priyambada L, Rewers A, Cherubini V, Estrada S, Wolfsdorf JI, Codner E. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state. Pediatric Diabetes 2022;23(7):835–856, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13406.*

This chapter is mainly about hospital treatment. Mellis is not a hospital tool, so this summary concentrates on what a family or an assistant needs: what DKA is, how it shows, the role of ketones, why it happens in people already on insulin (including pump infusion set failure) and when home care stops being appropriate. The hospital protocol is outlined only briefly. For home ketone management (extra insulin, fluids, AID) see the ISPAD 2022 sick day chapter (`ispad-2022-sick-day`). Grades A/B/C/E are the chapter's; E = expert consensus.

## Key points

- DKA needs all three: glucose above 11 mmol/L (≈200 mg/dL); venous pH below 7.3 or serum bicarbonate below 18 mmol/L (C); and ketonaemia (blood β-hydroxybutyrate, BOHB, ≥3 mmol/L) (C) or moderate/large ketonuria [Executive summary; Section 4].
- Severity follows the acidosis: mild pH <7.3 or bicarbonate <18 mmol/L; moderate pH <7.2 or bicarbonate <10; severe pH <7.1 or bicarbonate <5 [Section 4].
- BOHB ≥3 mmol/L is a sensitive DKA marker; ≥5.3 mmol/L is more specific (about 91% accuracy in hyperglycaemic children in emergency departments) [Section 4].
- Urine strips miss BOHB, the main DKA ketone, so urine testing alone can underestimate ketosis; expired or badly stored strips can read falsely negative [Section 4].
- DKA can occur with only modestly raised glucose ("euglycaemic ketoacidosis") after partial treatment, fasting, very low-carbohydrate/high-fat diets or SGLT2 inhibitor use [Section 4].
- In children who already have diabetes, DKA without a preceding illness almost always means insulin was missed or delivery was interrupted, most often by insulin pump infusion set problems (E) [Executive summary; Sections 5, 9].
- Recurrent DKA risk in established diabetes is about 1–10% per patient-year; infections (mainly gastroenteritis) cause a minority [Section 5].
- A child whose family is trained in sick-day care can be managed at home with subcutaneous insulin when there is hyperglycaemia and ketosis but no vomiting or severe dehydration [Section 6.2].
- Fingerstick glucose should be considered in any child with fast breathing, or with vomiting and abdominal pain without diarrhoea, because classic symptoms are not always reported [Executive summary].

## Definitions and thresholds

| Measure | DKA | HHS (hyperglycaemic hyperosmolar state) |
|---|---|---|
| Glucose | >11 mmol/L (≈200 mg/dL) | >33.3 mmol/L (600 mg/dL) |
| pH | venous <7.3 | venous >7.25, arterial >7.30 |
| Bicarbonate | <18 mmol/L | >15 mmol/L |
| Ketones | blood BOHB ≥3 mmol/L or moderate–large urine ketones (typically ≥2+) | small urine ketones, absent to mild blood ketones |
| Osmolality | — | effective osmolality >320 mOsm/kg |

[Executive summary; Section 4]. Bicarbonate can stand in for pH when pH is unavailable. Mixed presentations occur: some HHS patients have mild–moderate acidosis from poor perfusion, and some DKA patients have HHS-level glucose and osmolality ("hyperosmolar DKA"), which changes treatment [Sections 4, 10.3]. HHS develops with gradually increasing thirst and urination, is commoner in type 2 diabetes and has higher complication and death rates than DKA [Section 10].

Interferences with urine ketone tests: some sulfhydryl drugs (captopril, N-acetylcysteine, mesna, penicillamine) and valproic acid can give false positives [Section 4]. Point-of-care blood BOHB meters track a laboratory method well up to about 3 mmol/L but are not accurate above about 5 mmol/L [Section 7].

## How DKA develops

Absolute or relative lack of insulin together with high counter-regulatory hormones (glucagon, catecholamines, cortisol, growth hormone) drives glucose production up, glucose use down, and fat breakdown into ketoacids, causing ketonaemia and metabolic acidosis [Section 3]. Once glucose exceeds the renal threshold of about 10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL), osmotic diuresis loses water and electrolytes; vomiting worsens dehydration, which raises stress hormones further — a cycle that is fatal unless broken by insulin, fluid and electrolytes [Section 3].

Causes [Sections 3, 5]:
- new-onset diabetes (DKA at diagnosis occurs in roughly 15–70% of children in Europe and North America; delayed diagnosis increases the risk);
- missed insulin, especially the long-acting part of a basal-bolus regimen, or doses cut sharply during an illness such as gastroenteritis;
- interruption of delivery in pump users — DKA can come on fast because there is no long-acting depot;
- relative insulin deficiency in sepsis, trauma or febrile illness despite usual doses;
- drinking large amounts of sugary drinks can worsen hyperglycaemia before presentation.

## Warning signs

Clinical picture of DKA [Section 3]: dehydration; rapid breathing, deep sighing (Kussmaul) respiration; nausea, vomiting and abdominal pain that can mimic an acute abdomen; confusion and drowsiness. Blood pressure is often normal or even high despite dehydration, so normal blood pressure is not reassuring [Sections 3, 6.1].

Signs of marked dehydration [Section 6.1]: capillary refill longer than 2 s, poor skin turgor, dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, no tears, weak pulses, cool hands and feet. Weak or absent pulses, low blood pressure or low urine output suggest dehydration of 10% or more.

Cerebral injury (cerebral oedema) is the main cause of DKA deaths. Clinically obvious injury occurs in about 0.5–0.9% of DKA episodes with 21–24% mortality; it usually starts within the first 12 h of treatment but can occur before treatment or, rarely, 24–48 h later [Section 8.2]. Warning signs (C) [Executive summary; Section 8.2]: new or worsening headache or vomiting after treatment begins, slowing heart rate not explained by sleep or improved circulation, changes in behaviour or consciousness (irritability, lethargy, confusion, incontinence), cranial nerve signs, falling oxygen saturation. Higher risk: age under 5, new-onset diabetes, longer symptoms, severe acidosis (pH <7.1), very low pCO2 (<21 mmHg) and raised urea nitrogen (>20 mg/dL) [Executive summary; Sections 6.2, 8.2]. High blood pressure alone is not a warning sign [Executive summary].

Even a single DKA episode has been linked to subtle memory changes after diagnosis; kidney injury is common in hospitalised DKA (43–64%) [Section 8.1].

## Ketones: blood versus urine

- Blood BOHB should be measured whenever possible, both to confirm DKA (≥3 mmol/L in children) and to follow recovery [Sections 4, 6.1].
- With adequate insulin, BOHB should fall by about 0.5 mmol/L per hour in hospital treatment [Section 6.4].
- Urine ketones (nitroprusside reaction: acetoacetate and acetone) stay positive for several hours after blood BOHB has normalised, so absent ketonuria must not be used to judge that DKA has resolved [Section 6.6].
- In hospital, a persistently low bicarbonate can come from hyperchloraemia (from chloride-rich fluids) rather than ongoing ketosis; bedside BOHB (or anion gap) decides whether ketoacidosis is really over [Sections 6.3.3, 6.5].

## Prevention, pumps and recurrent DKA

- Most DKA in children with known diabetes is due to insulin omission, inadvertent or deliberate, or interrupted pump delivery [Sections 5, 9].
- Families with repeated DKA should review sick-day and pump-failure procedures with a diabetes professional; when deliberate omission is suspected, a social worker or psychologist should help find the reasons [Section 9].
- Treatment of an episode is not complete until its cause has been looked for and addressed (E) [Executive summary].
- For new-onset diabetes, awareness campaigns about diabetes symptoms have reduced DKA at diagnosis [Section 5].
- The chapter does not give a step-by-step pump troubleshooting list; those steps (check line, site and cannula, change the set, give extra insulin by pen) are in the sick day chapter [see `ispad-2022-sick-day`, Section 9.1].

## Home or hospital

Home or outpatient care with subcutaneous insulin and an experienced diabetes team is possible for a child with known diabetes and trained parents when there is hyperglycaemia and ketosis without vomiting or severe dehydration [Section 6.2]. Otherwise DKA belongs in a centre experienced with children's DKA, with frequent checks of vital signs, neurology and laboratory values (E); smaller centres should have phone or video support from an expert (E) [Executive summary; Section 6.2]. Severe DKA (long symptoms, poor circulation, reduced consciousness) or high cerebral-injury risk calls for intensive care or an equivalent unit [Section 6.2].

## Hospital treatment in brief

Only an outline; this is for understanding, not for use [Executive summary; Sections 6.3–6.6, 7]:
- Emergency assessment along paediatric life support lines: glucose, ketones, electrolytes, blood gases, consciousness (Glasgow coma scale), two IV lines (E).
- Fluids before insulin: 0.9% saline 10–20 ml/kg over 20–30 min (E), then 0.45–0.9% saline or balanced solutions replacing an assumed 5–10% deficit over 24–48 h plus maintenance (A). The PECARN FLUID trial found no difference in brain outcomes between faster and slower rehydration.
- Insulin infusion 0.05–0.1 U/kg/h starting at least 1 h after fluids begin, no IV bolus (B); 0.05 U/kg/h may suffice when pH >7.15. Dextrose is added when glucose falls to about 14–17 mmol/L (250–300 mg/dL). In uncomplicated mild–moderate DKA, hourly or 2-hourly subcutaneous rapid-acting analogue can be an alternative where IV infusion is impossible.
- Potassium is replaced in almost all cases, guided by levels; bicarbonate is not recommended except for life-threatening hyperkalaemia or pH <6.9 with poor cardiac function (C).
- DKA counts as resolved at roughly pH >7.30, bicarbonate >18 mmol/L, BOHB <1 mmol/L or a closed anion gap; the first subcutaneous rapid-acting dose is given 15–30 min before the infusion stops, with longer overlap for basal insulin.
- HHS differs: more fluid, insulin started later and lower (0.025–0.05 U/kg/h once glucose falls by less than 3 mmol/L [50 mg/dL] per hour on fluid alone) (C).

## Limits of this summary

- The chapter covers children and adolescents; adult DKA protocols differ in places.
- Evidence is mixed: a few A/B grades (fluid replacement, insulin start), many C and E (consensus) recommendations.
- Hospital fluid, electrolyte, cerebral-oedema and HHS treatment are condensed or left out, as are Tables 1–2 and the algorithms.
- Suspected DKA is an emergency; read the original and contact the care team or emergency services for clinical decisions.
