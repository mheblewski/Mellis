This is a factual account of the targets the chapter sets, not an adaptation of its text. Grades A/B/E are the chapter's (E = expert consensus).

## Key points

- HbA1c target below 53 mmol/mol (7.0%) for young people with diabetes (A) [Executive summary; Section 4.1.1].
- A lower target below 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) during remission ("honeymoon") or early stage 3 diabetes, and where CGM or automated insulin delivery (AID) are combined with a highly skilled diabetes team (E) [Executive summary; Section 4.1.1].
- HbA1c every 3 months, i.e. at least four times a year (E) [Executive summary; Section 4.1.2].
- CGM targets over 14 days (B): time in range (TIR) 3.9–10 mmol/L (70–180 mg/dL) above 70%; below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) under 4%; below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL) under 1%; above 10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL) under 25%; above 13.9 mmol/L (250 mg/dL) under 5%; coefficient of variation (%CV) 36% or less [Executive summary; Section 4.2.1].
- Fingerstick (SMBG) target 4–10 mmol/L (70–180 mg/dL), with fasting values of 4–8 mmol/L (70–144 mg/dL) (E); at least 6 checks a day for people on insulin who use SMBG (B) [Executive summary; Section 4.3.1].
- Looser targets only when reaching the standard ones would harm overall well-being (for example severe anxiety, or no access to analogues, AID or enough strips/CGM) (E) [Executive summary; Section 3].
- Reaching targets lowers acute and chronic complication risk (A) and limits effects of highs and lows on brain development, cognition, mood and quality of life (B) [Executive summary].

The chapter applies these to children, adolescents and young adults under 25 [Section 1].

## Why one target, and when to individualise

ISPAD replaced its earlier meal- and time-specific glucose ranges with a single SMBG range that matches the CGM time-in-range band, judging that detailed ranges had no evidence of benefit and risked mixed messages [Sections 1, 4.3.1]. The team's job, for most young people, is to build an individual care plan that reaches the standard targets rather than to lower the bar [Section 3]. With modern intensive treatment, lower HbA1c is no longer linked to more severe hypoglycaemia, so outside limited-resource settings fear of severe lows is usually not a reason for a higher HbA1c target [Section 3]. Exceptions include major psychological burden, limited lifespan, neonatal diabetes and settings where the targets are unreachable [Section 3]. A team that agrees on targets and speaks with "one voice" gets better results (B); benchmarking between centres helps (B); improving access to care, insulin and technology increases the share reaching targets (A) [Executive summary].

## HbA1c: what it reflects and its limits

HbA1c reflects average glucose over roughly 8–12 weeks, weighted to recent weeks: about 50% from the last 30 days, 40% from days 31–90 and 10% from days 91–120 [Section 4.1.2]. Risk reduction continues as HbA1c approaches about 42 mmol/mol (6%), but gains are smaller below 53 mmol/mol (7%) [Section 4.1.1]. HbA1c can mislead [Section 4.1.3; Table 1]:
- conditions with faster red cell turnover (recovery from iron/B12/folate deficiency, blood loss, haemolysis such as sickle cell or thalassaemia, cystic fibrosis, chemotherapy, kidney disease on erythropoietin or dialysis, second-trimester pregnancy) give falsely low values;
- slower turnover (iron/B12/folate deficiency, third-trimester pregnancy, uraemia) gives falsely high values;
- the same HbA1c can hide very different variability, and variability predicts severe hypoglycaemia.
When HbA1c is unreliable and there is no CGM, fructosamine (about 2–3 weeks) or 1,5-anhydroglucitol (about 2–14 days) are alternatives [Section 4.1.3]. The CGM-derived Glucose Management Indicator (GMI) does not always match laboratory HbA1c, so calling it an "estimated HbA1c" should be avoided [Section 4.1.3].

## CGM: metrics, accuracy and practicalities

- Targets come from the standardised international CGM consensus and are reported in the ambulatory glucose profile (AGP); use them together with HbA1c where both are available [Section 4.2.1].
- People without diabetes may spend around 3.2% of time below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) depending on sensor accuracy, but rarely go below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL); cutting time below 3.0 mmol/L matters most [Section 4.2.2].
- Several CGM and intermittently scanned CGM systems are approved for non-adjunctive use, yet the chapter still recommends confirming hypoglycaemia with a fingerstick, and checking when symptoms do not fit the sensor reading [Section 4.2.2].
- Skin irritation is the commonest reason for stopping CGM; alarm fatigue also contributes, so alarms should be introduced in a person-centred way [Section 4.2.2].
- Early CGM use from diagnosis is linked to better long-term HbA1c; access is unequal [Section 4.2.2].

## SMBG details

Bedtime readings above 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) are acceptable, though families may prefer higher values within 4–10 mmol/L after a recent low, around exercise, with hypoglycaemia unawareness or without CGM low alarms [Section 4.3.1]. Exercise glucose ranges are left to the ISPAD exercise chapter [Section 4.3.1].

## Development stages

HbA1c tends to rise and TIR fall as the honeymoon ends; targets should be reinforced then because the early HbA1c course predicts the long-term one [Section 5]. Pre-schoolers have higher variability from unpredictable eating and activity; adolescence brings insulin resistance and rising HbA1c in registries [Section 5].

## Limits of this summary

- Written for children, adolescents and young adults under 25; adult targets from other bodies may differ.
- CGM time-in-range targets are grade B; the SMBG ranges and the lower 6.5% HbA1c target are expert consensus (E).
- Sections on social determinants of health, education strategies and type 2 diabetes are summarised briefly or left out.
- Targets should be agreed with the person's own diabetes team; read the original for clinical decisions.
