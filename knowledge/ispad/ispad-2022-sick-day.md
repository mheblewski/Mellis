## Key points

- Insulin must never be stopped completely during illness; it may need to go up or down. Replace pen needle/cartridge or pump cartridge, tubing and cannula to make sure insulin is really being delivered (grade B) [Executive summary; Section 4.5].
- Check glucose (fingerstick or CGM) every 1–2 h during illness (E); the executive summary says ketones at least every 1–2 h as well, while Section 6.4 gives every 2–4 h for ketones in mild illness [Executive summary; Sections 4.2, 6.4].
- Blood ketone (β-hydroxybutyrate, BOHB) testing is preferred to urine ketones; it reduces emergency visits and admissions (B) [Executive summary; Section 4.3].
- Sick-day aims: glucose 3.9–10 mmol/L (70–180 mg/dL) and blood ketones below 0.6 mmol/L (E) [Executive summary].
- Blood BOHB of 0.6 mmol/L or more is abnormal in a child with diabetes [Section 6.4].
- Fluids: carbohydrate-containing drinks when glucose is below 14 mmol/L (250 mg/dL), carbohydrate-free drinks above it; with ketosis aim for about 4–6 ml/kg/h in small sips every 5–10 min [Executive summary; Section 8.2].
- Extra insulin for ketosis is given as ONE of three alternative methods: 10–20% of total daily dose (TDD), 0.1–0.15 U/kg, or the usual correction dose increased by 10% (mild ketosis) or 20% (moderate/severe) [Section 8.3; Figure 3].
- BOHB 3.0 mmol/L or more (or large urine ketones) suggests possible DKA: go to an emergency department [Sections 8.1, 9.1].
- Pump users: DKA can develop within hours when delivery fails. With glucose ≥14 mmol/L (250 mg/dL), check pump, line and site, change the set, and give the first extra doses by pen or syringe [Sections 6.3, 9.1].
- Hybrid closed loop / AID: if ketones are 0.6 mmol/L or higher, or vomiting occurs, stop closed loop and use open-loop/manual mode with the usual sick-day rules [Executive summary; Section 9.3].
- Gastroenteritis is the most common cause of hypoglycaemia on sick days; total daily insulin may be reduced by 20–50%, but insulin is never stopped (E) [Executive summary; Section 7.2].
- Mini-dose glucagon for persistent lows with vomiting or food refusal: 0.02 mg up to age 2, then 0.01 mg per year of age, maximum 0.15 mg; may be repeated after 30–60 min [Table 1; Section 7.3].

## Why illness changes glucose and ketones

Fever and most infections raise glucose through stress (counter-regulatory) hormones that increase glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis and insulin resistance, and they push ketone production up when insulin is short [Section 3]. Vomiting and diarrhoea illnesses (viral gastroenteritis) work the other way: poor intake, slow gastric emptying and malabsorption make hypoglycaemia more likely [Section 3]. Insulin needs can climb during the incubation days before symptoms and stay up for a few days after recovery, and they vary a lot between people and illnesses [Section 3].

Specific patterns named in the chapter [Section 3]:
- COVID-19 infection, and even COVID-19 vaccination, can raise insulin needs for days or weeks; up to 2.2 U/kg/day has been needed at the inflammatory peak, with a quick reduction needed on recovery.
- Steroid treatment and the days around menstruation can bring predictable rises in insulin needs.
- In coeliac disease, a gluten exposure can cause prolonged hyperglycaemia (sometimes overnight) needing sick-day doses.
- Children in the partial remission ("honeymoon") phase may need considerably larger relative increases because their usual doses are small (E) [Executive summary].

## Monitoring: glucose, CGM and ketones

**Glucose.** Monitor every 1–2 h, with adult supervision even for adolescents [Section 4.2]. CGM is useful because it shows whether glucose is rising, falling or flat, but its accuracy can suffer during illness: some drugs used on sick days (acetaminophen/paracetamol, ascorbic acid, salicylic acid) can interfere with some sensors, and poor perfusion from dehydration can reduce accuracy. In those cases fingerstick checks are still needed; when the person feels unwell, capillary readings are desirable [Sections 4.2, 4.6, 6.4]. Some newer sensors are not affected by acetaminophen [Section 4.6]. Remote data sharing with the family and the team is encouraged [Sections 1, 4.2].

**Ketones.** The three ketone bodies are acetoacetate, acetone and β-hydroxybutyrate (BOHB); BOHB dominates in DKA [Section 4.3]. Points the chapter makes in favour of blood testing [Sections 4.3, 6.4, 8.1]:
- Urine strips detect acetoacetate (and acetone with glycine strips), not BOHB, so urine can read negative or only trace/small while blood BOHB is already high.
- After a pump stop, ketosis shows up later in urine than in blood.
- During recovery, urine ketones stay positive for hours (sometimes more than 24 h) after blood BOHB has normalised; dosing insulin by urine results at that stage can cause hypoglycaemia.
- In response to extra insulin, BOHB may rise by about 10–20% in the first 1–2 h but should be lower by 2 h.
- Opened urine strip bottles can deteriorate within about a month; foil-wrapped strips are preferred. Blood testing helps in small children who cannot give urine on demand.

**Ketone bands used in the chapter** [Sections 6.4, 8.1, 8.2; Table 2; Figure 3]:

| Blood BOHB | Meaning in the chapter |
|---|---|
| below 0.6 mmol/L | normal; the sick-day target |
| 0.6 mmol/L or more | abnormal in diabetes; action needed |
| below ~0.9 mmol/L or trace urine | may be starvation ketosis |
| 0.6–1.5 mmol/L (urine small–moderate) | mild ketosis (Figure 3 band) |
| 1.0–2.9 mmol/L | may be treated at home |
| 1.5–2.9 mmol/L (urine moderate–large) | moderate ketosis (Figure 3 band) |
| above 1.5 mmol/L persisting despite extra insulin and fluids | seek urgent review |
| 3.0 mmol/L or more (urine large) | possible DKA; emergency department. Starvation ketosis can occasionally exceed 3 mmol/L, which needs venous pH to tell apart |

## Hyperglycaemia without ketones: preventing ketosis (Figure 2, Section 6.2)

General rules from the text [Section 6.2]: give the usual carbohydrate and correction dose, and repeat the correction after 2 h if needed. Basal insulin (long-acting, intermediate, or pump basal rate) may need a 20–30% increase depending on how high glucose runs. For post-meal highs, raise the calculated bolus by 10% (mild) or 20% (moderate to large).

Our layout of the Figure 2 bands (as restated in the earlier figures file):

| Glucose | Insulin | Fluids |
|---|---|---|
| below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) | — | carbohydrate-containing drinks; consider mini-dose glucagon if lows persist |
| 3.9–10 mmol/L (70–180 mg/dL) | usual bolus from carb ratio (ICR) and ISF | carbohydrate-containing drinks, about 100 ml/h |
| 10–14 mmol/L (180–250 mg/dL) | ICR/ISF bolus; correction may be repeated every 2 h; consider +10% for persistent post-meal highs | carbohydrate-containing, 4–6 ml/kg/h |
| 14–22 mmol/L (250–400 mg/dL) | as above; consider +10–20% | sugar-free, 4–6 ml/kg/h |
| above 22 mmol/L (400 mg/dL) | ICR/ISF bolus plus 10%; correction every 2 h; consider +10–20% | sugar-free, 4–6 ml/kg/h |

At every level: check ketones every 2–4 h. When high glucose persists or illness is expected to last 3 days or more, basal insulin is increased and reduced again gradually as the illness settles. The chapter gives slightly different figures in different places: 20–30% in Section 6.2; 10–20% in the Table 2 notes, with pump basal up to 50% if needed (E); the figures file restated 20–30% for injected basal and 20–50% for pump basal. ISF should be recalculated daily during illness because resistance changes [Table 2 notes].

## Hyperglycaemia with ketones: extra insulin at home (Figure 3, Section 8.3)

Supplemental doses are ultra-rapid, rapid-acting or short-acting insulin, repeated every 1–2 h when ketosis is severe and every 2–4 h when mild, guided by glucose and ketone results [Section 8.3]. The chapter describes several methods used around the world; they are alternatives for working out one supplemental dose, not doses to add together:

| Ketone level | Method A: correction dose increased | Method B: body weight | Method C: % of TDD | Also |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| mild, BOHB 0.6–1.5 mmol/L | usual correction × 1.1 (+10%) | 0.1 U/kg | 10% of TDD | fluids 4–6 ml/kg/h; recheck glucose and ketones in 2 h |
| moderate, BOHB 1.5–2.9 mmol/L | usual correction × 1.2 (+20%) | 0.15 U/kg | 20% of TDD | as above |
| BOHB ≥3.0 mmol/L | not a home situation: transfer to hospital (possible DKA) | | | |

Details and caveats [Section 8.3; Table 2 notes]:
- Worked example in the text: a correction that would normally be 5 U becomes 6 U with moderate ketones (+20%).
- If ketosis does not improve with the percentage method, 150–200% of the calculated correction, repeated every 2–4 h by response, is mentioned.
- 0.1–0.15 U/kg suits children on roughly 0.7–1.0 U/kg/day. With low usual needs (<0.7 U/kg/day) or high needs/insulin resistance (>1 U/kg/day), the percentage methods may fit better.
- In the honeymoon phase, the standard 10–20% of TDD may be too little; doses of about 0.05–0.1 U/kg with assessment of the response are suggested.
- TDD = all insulin on a usual day (rapid/short plus long/intermediate, or pump basal plus boluses), including correction boluses.
- After 2 h: ketones negative → continue the no-ketone plan above; still positive → repeat one supplemental dose; BOHB staying above 1.5 mmol/L means high DKA risk, and with vomiting, transfer is advised (figures file restatement).

## Normal or low glucose with ketones (starvation ketosis, Table 2)

Ketones with low or normal glucose usually mean too little energy intake; the treatment is carbohydrate plus insulin, not withholding insulin (E) [Executive summary; Section 7.2]. Table 2 (p. 920) crosses blood ketone bands with glucose below 5.0 mmol/L (<90 mg/dL) or 5.0–10 mmol/L (90–180 mg/dL). The overall pattern:
- Low ketones and low glucose: no extra insulin, total daily dose reduced by about 20%, sugary fluids and extra carbohydrate; treat glucose below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) as a hypo and consider mini-dose glucagon.
- As ketones rise, the dose reduction shrinks (about 15% at 0.6–0.9 mmol/L) and stops (do not reduce TDD at 1.5–2.9 mmol/L); a correction by ISF is given once glucose has risen above about 5–6 mmol/L (90–110 mg/dL).
- With normal glucose, the usual bolus is given, and at higher ketone levels about 5% of TDD or 0.05 U/kg is added to it.
- At 3 mmol/L or more there is a risk of ketoacidosis; if the child is vomiting or cannot eat or drink, IV saline with 5% glucose may be needed.
- Check glucose and ketones every 2 h. "Starvation" blood ketones are usually below 3.0 mmol/L. When ketones are elevated, giving extra insulin is the priority even if glucose is low; when ketones are negative/low and glucose is below 10–14 mmol/L, the child should sip sugary fluids (at least ~100 ml/h).

## Fluids and food

Hyperglycaemia, fever, glycosuria and ketonuria all increase fluid losses; preventing dehydration is a priority [Sections 4.4, 6.5]. Practical points [Sections 6.5, 8.2]: small sips of cool drinks are better tolerated when vomiting; ice lollies can help (sugar-free when glucose is high, sugary when below 14 mmol/L / 250 mg/dL); easily digested carbohydrate foods (rice porridge, crackers, noodles, yoghurt, bananas, etc.) can replace meals when appetite is poor, as long as insulin is given; removing fizz from soft drinks may reduce bloating. Keep a record of intake, watch urine output and weigh every 4–6 h if possible: steady weight is reassuring, continuing weight loss means contacting the team. With ketosis, about 4–6 ml/kg/h or 100 ml/h is suggested; above 14 mmol/L the drinks should contain salt but no glucose [Section 8.2]. Seek urgent advice if the child cannot drink; consider IV fluids early (E) [Executive summary].

## Vomiting and gastroenteritis

Treat nausea or vomiting as a sign of insulin deficiency and ketosis until proven otherwise (E) [Executive summary; Section 7.1]. Other causes are the illness itself (gastroenteritis, food poisoning, appendicitis and so on) or severe hypoglycaemia. With high glucose and ketones, extra insulin is needed even while vomiting, and vomiting may settle once ketosis is treated [Section 7.1].

For gastroenteritis with lows [Sections 7.2, 9.2]: reduce total daily insulin by 20–50%, typically starting with about 20% less basal and 50% less bolus, and the bolus may be given after eating to be sure the food or drink went down. Pump basal may be cut by 20–50% as a temporary basal for 2–4 h or longer. If ketones appear, the reduction has gone too far. Lows can start before the first vomit and continue for days afterwards. Antiemetics (e.g. ondansetron) may be considered in gastroenteritis if not contraindicated, but not when mental status is altered or when vomiting is due to DKA [Section 7.1].

Mini-dose glucagon (Table 1, p. 916; Section 7.3) — for glucose staying below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) with vomiting or food refusal; much smaller than the emergency severe-hypo dose:

| Age | Dose | On a U-100 insulin syringe |
|---|---|---|
| under 2 years | 0.02 mg (20 µg) | 2 units |
| 2–15 years | 0.01 mg (10 µg) per year of age | 1 unit per year |
| over 15 years | 0.15 mg (150 µg) | 15 units |

It can be repeated after 30–60 min; if lows persist or glucagon is unavailable, IV dextrose via emergency services is needed [Section 7.3].

## Pumps, hybrid closed loop and AID

- No long-acting depot: pump users rely only on rapid/short insulin, so DKA develops quickly when delivery is interrupted or during illness; BOHB rises can come before urine ketones [Sections 6.3, 9.1].
- At glucose 14 mmol/L (250 mg/dL) or above, look for kinks, air in the line, leaks at connections, a dislodged cannula and site inflammation; replace cartridge, needle, tubing and cannula [Section 9.1].
- Give the first extra boluses by pen or syringe rather than through the pump, so delivery is certain. If ketones have not fallen after the expected early rise, repeat the dose with insulin from a new cartridge or vial [Section 9.1].
- Temporary basal +20–50% or more until glucose improves and BOHB is below 0.6 mmol/L (or urine negative/small); the pump's maximum basal limit may need raising [Section 9.1].
- AID systems with adjustable targets and user modes can help keep glucose in range on sick days, but "if in doubt" manual mode may be better. Corrections during illness are raised by 10–20% and can go through the pump once the set has been changed. With ketones ≥0.6 mmol/L or vomiting, stop closed loop and follow open-loop sick-day rules to make sure enough supplemental insulin is delivered [Executive summary; Section 9.3].
- Pumps and closed loop can continue in hospital when staff know the technology, supplies are available and the person can operate it safely; reasons to stop include pump malfunction, lack of supplies, reduced consciousness and suicide risk [Section 9.4].

## When to seek medical help

Medical review (E) [Executive summary]: the child gets worse; the cause of illness is unclear; fever persists; language or understanding problems; the family lacks resources to manage at home; co-morbidities (e.g. Down syndrome, eating disorders, mental illness, epilepsy, inflammatory bowel disease, malaria); age under 5; glucose cannot be kept above 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL).

Urgent emergency review (E) [Executive summary; Sections 7.1, 8.1, 9.1]:
- vomiting lasting more than 2 h, especially in young children;
- continuing weight loss suggesting worsening dehydration or poor circulation;
- glucose keeps rising despite extra insulin, or lows cannot be corrected;
- fruity (acetone) breath appears or worsens;
- blood ketones stay above 1.5 mmol/L, or urine ketones stay large, despite extra insulin and fluids; BOHB ≥3 mmol/L;
- exhaustion, confusion, deep rapid (Kussmaul) breathing, severe abdominal pain, or any change in neurological status (loss of consciousness, seizures).

## Preparation, supplies and other risks

Sick-day education should start at diagnosis in a simple form (monitor often, never stop insulin, call early), be repeated at least yearly and tailored to age (C) [Executive summary; Section 5.1]. A home kit should include glucose and ketone strips, insulin, emergency glucagon (or nasal glucagon), and a written or electronic plan covering targets, dose changes, fluids, ketone response, pump failure, mini-dose glucagon and whom to call [Section 5.2]. Phone access to the team reduces deterioration and admissions [Section 5.3]; influenza and COVID vaccination per local advice [Section 5.4]. Check that insulin has not been heat- or freeze-damaged [Section 6.1].

SGLT2 (or SGLT1/2) inhibitors raise DKA risk, including DKA with near-normal glucose (euglycaemic DKA); stop them when unwell or when ketones appear [Section 10]. Low-carbohydrate diets carry a high risk of hyperketonaemia during illness; more blood ketone monitoring may reduce it [Section 11].

## Limits of this summary

- The guideline is written for children and adolescents; adult use is not its scope.
- Most recommendations are expert consensus (grade E); only a few carry B or C grades.
- The Figure 2/3 tables above come from an earlier restatement of image-only figures plus the chapter text; exact cell placement in Table 2 was hard to read in the extracted text, so only its overall pattern is given. Hospital management, references and some country-specific advice are left out.
- Read the original and follow the person's own care team plan for clinical decisions.
