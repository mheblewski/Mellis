*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Tauschmann M, Forlenza G, Hood K, et al. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Diabetes technologies: Glucose monitoring. Pediatr Diabetes. 2022;23(8):1390-1405, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13451.*

## Key points

- CGM is strongly recommended for all children, adolescents and young adults with T1D [grade A], and should be started as soon as possible after diagnosis where available [grade B] [Executive summary].
- Real-time CGM (rtCGM) lowers HbA1c, raises time in range (TIR), reduces mild-to-moderate hypoglycaemia and glucose variability, on pumps, closed loop and MDI alike; benefit depends on how much of the time the sensor is worn [grade A] [Executive summary].
- Intermittently scanned CGM (isCGM, "flash") is safe and may improve TIR and HbA1c; 11–13 scans/day go with better results [grade B].
- Interstitial glucose lags blood glucose by about **5–10 min**; sensor errors are largest in the hypoglycaemic range and when glucose changes quickly [Accuracy of CGM devices].
- Current sensors reach a **MARD of about 8–10%** in trials with about 99% of readings in error-grid zones A+B; MARD tends to be higher at home than in clinic [Accuracy].
- A MARD of ≤10% has been modelled as safe for **non-adjunctive use** (dosing from CGM without a fingerstick) [Non-adjuvant use].
- Still use a fingerstick in hypoglycaemia, when glucose changes fast, and whenever symptoms do not match the CGM value [Non-adjuvant use; Sensor interference].
- Several common drugs can bias some sensors: acetaminophen (paracetamol) 1000 mg, vitamin C ≥500 mg (falsely high), salicylic acid ≥650 mg (slightly low), hydroxyurea (markedly high) [Sensor interference].
- With fingersticks only, 6–10 checks a day may be needed [grade B]; use only meters meeting ISO 15197:2013 or FDA standards [grade E] [Executive summary].

## Fingerstick blood glucose (SMBG) and meter accuracy

- ISO 15197:2013 (home use): 95% of results within 15 mg/dL (0.83 mmol/L) when glucose is below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), and within 15% at or above 100 mg/dL; 99% in zones A/B of the consensus error grid. FDA (home use): 95% within 15% and 99% within 20% across the usable range [Table 1].
- Real-world meter accuracy can be worse than in testing [Meter standards].
- Accuracy needs washed and fully dried hands, a correct blood sample and stored, in-date, genuine strips. Meters work only within their temperature range.
- Glucose-oxidase meters depend on oxygen: low oxygen (altitude, hypoxia, venous blood) gives falsely high readings, high oxygen (arterial blood) falsely low. Uric acid and acetaminophen lower readings; L-DOPA, vitamin C and tolazamide have variable effects. Glucose-dehydrogenase meters read falsely high with galactose or xylose [Table 2].
- Meters with built-in bolus calculators helped more people reach HbA1c targets and reduced hypoglycaemia in trials [Expert meters].
- When to test with an intensive regimen: before meals and snacks; 2–3 h after meals to judge meal doses; to confirm a low and follow recovery; at bedtime, overnight as needed and on waking; before and during hazardous tasks such as driving; before, during and for several hours after vigorous exercise; during illness [Frequency and timing of SMBG testing].

## Types of CGM

- **Professional (blinded) CGM:** data hidden from the user, reviewed by clinicians.
- **Real-time CGM:** values shown at set intervals, with high/low and rate-of-change alarms, phone apps and cloud sharing for remote monitoring. Transdermal sensors last 6–14 days; an implanted fluorescence sensor (Eversense) lasts up to 6 months (180 days in the newer version, daily calibration) and is approved only for adults.
- **Intermittently scanned CGM:** shows glucose only when scanned. FreeStyle Libre gives readings for up to 14 days after a **1 h warm-up**. Libre 2 has optional alarms and a MARD of 9.2% (adults) and 9.7% (children); Libre 3 works as a real-time CGM [Categories of sensors].

## CGM accuracy, MARD and lag time

- Why CGM and blood glucose differ: physiological delay of about 5–10 min for glucose to move from blood to interstitial fluid; the sensor's own reaction time; digital smoothing filters; and mechanical factors such as small movements and **pressure on the sensor** [Accuracy of CGM devices].
- **MARD** = average absolute difference between sensor and reference values as a percentage of the reference. Lower is better, but MARD alone is not enough to judge a sensor. Error-grid analysis shows how clinically important the differences are (zones A and B acceptable).
- Until recently there were no fixed minimum accuracy standards for CGM; the FDA now has an "integrated CGM" (iCGM) pathway with accuracy rules for sensors used with pumps and automated insulin dosing [Accuracy].

## Sensor interference (interfering substances)

- Hydroxyurea in therapeutic doses can raise sensor readings markedly.
- Acetaminophen 1000 mg can falsely raise readings on some systems.
- Salicylic acid ≥650 mg may lower readings slightly; ascorbic acid (vitamin C) ≥500 mg may raise them.
- Lisinopril, albuterol, atenolol and red wine may also affect readings.
- Enzyme-based electrochemical sensors are more prone to interference than fluorescence sensors (for the implanted sensor only tetracycline and mannitol caused relevant bias in vitro).
- These substances are often in over-the-counter combination products or supplements, so people may not know they took them. The bias matters most for people dosing from CGM without fingersticks and for **closed-loop** systems that dose from CGM. Check with a meter whenever symptoms do not fit the CGM reading [Sensor interference].

## Calibration, factory calibration and compression lows

- Dexcom G6, Dexcom G7, Guardian 4 and FreeStyle Libre 1/2 are **factory-calibrated**; user calibration is generally not needed, removing a source of error. Manual calibration remains possible on rtCGM if sensor and meter disagree for a long time [Calibrations].
- Older sensors needing calibration: first calibration 1–2 h after insertion, then at least every 12 h (a schedule of three times a day, e.g. before breakfast, dinner and bed, is suggested in Table 3).
- Calibrate only when glucose is stable (before meals, before bed, before insulin, flat trend arrow).
- Calibrating while the signal is temporarily wrong, e.g. from an interfering substance or from pressure on the site (**"compression lows"**), makes later readings wrong [Calibrations].
- Falsely low alerts from compression can also cause needless alarm in remote followers [Remote monitoring].

## Non-adjunctive use and when to use a fingerstick

- Dexcom G5/G6: approved for non-adjunctive use from age 2; FreeStyle Libre from age 4; Guardian 4 (CE) from age 7 [Non-adjuvant use].
- The REPLACE-BG study supported the safety of non-adjunctive use.
- A fingerstick is still advised: to confirm hypoglycaemia; when glucose is changing fast; when symptoms and readings disagree; when manual calibration is needed; and when no CGM data are available — including for users of AID with factory-calibrated sensors [Non-adjuvant use; Conclusions].

## Evidence on CGM benefit

- Early CGM trials showed little benefit in youth, mainly because sensors were worn under half of the time; wearing ≥6 days/week gave benefit in all ages [Real-time CGM systems].
- Newer rtCGM: lower HbA1c, higher TIR, less hypoglycaemia (including severe) and less variability; similar gains on pumps and MDI. Registry data show fewer DKA episodes with CGM, but results on severe hypoglycaemia are mixed.
- In very young children (mean age 5.7 years) non-adjunctive rtCGM did not significantly raise TIR but did reduce hypoglycaemia.
- Starting CGM in the first year after diagnosis was linked to 0.66% lower HbA1c at 12 months and better HbA1c over 7 years [CGM use from diabetes onset].
- rtCGM beat isCGM for TIR and time in hypoglycaemia in the (few, mostly older-device) comparison studies [Rt CGM versus isCGM].

## CGM metrics

- TIR, the percentage of time with sensor glucose 70–180 mg/dL (3.9–10.0 mmol/L), is now used as a clinical marker and trial outcome that complements or replaces HbA1c; targets are in the ISPAD glycemic targets chapter [Conclusions].

## Alerts, exercise and remote monitoring

- Starting alerts (Table 3): consider leaving alerts off at first to avoid alarm fatigue; then use personal, wide thresholds, e.g. 70–250 mg/dL (3.9–13.9 mmol/L). Set the low alert first for people with recurrent lows, the high alert first for those with high glucose. Add rate-of-change or predictive alerts later, or in situations with fast changes (more activity, unusual foods).
- Education should cover sensor lag, trend arrows and dose adjustments based on sensor values and arrows [Table 3].
- **Exercise:** rtCGM helps prevent and detect exercise-related lows; predictive and rate-of-change alerts allow early action during and after exercise, and low thresholds help decide on carbohydrate based on trend arrows. In one adult RCT rtCGM was better than isCGM during exercise. Glucose ranges for exercise should be individualised (ISPAD exercise chapter) [Exercise].
- **Remote monitoring** improves parents' sleep, quality of life and stress, and can prevent prolonged night-time lows; it can also cause conflict (youth feeling "policed") and false alarms, so families should agree when carers step in [Remote monitoring].
- **Skin:** reactions to adhesives (often acrylates), eczema, pigmentation and itching limit long-term use, especially in small children; site care, removal technique and barrier products help [CGM and skin issues].

## Limits of this summary

- Population: children, adolescents and young adults; device approvals and names reflect 2022 and differ by country.
- Most CGM benefit statements are grade A/B; practical advice (alerts, calibration) is expert opinion.
- Psychosocial, telemedicine and access sections are only briefly covered; detailed CGM target values are in the separate glycemic targets chapter.
- For clinical decisions, read the original guideline and the device manufacturer's instructions.
