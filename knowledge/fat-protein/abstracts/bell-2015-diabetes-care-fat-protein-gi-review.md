# Impact of fat, protein, and glycemic index on postprandial glucose control in type 1 diabetes: implications for intensive diabetes management in the continuous glucose monitoring era (systematic review)

- **Authors:** Bell KJ, Smart CE, Steil GM, Brand-Miller JC, King B, Wolpert HA
- **Journal/year:** Diabetes Care 2015;38(6):1008-1015
- **DOI:** 10.2337/dc15-0100
- **PubMed:** https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25998293/
- **Licence:** not open access (or open-access status not confirmed). This file contains a paraphrased summary of the abstract and key numbers written for the Mellis corpus, not the publisher's text.

## Summary of the abstract (paraphrased)

- **Design:** systematic review (MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Cochrane CENTRAL) of studies on how dietary fat, protein and glycaemic index (GI) change acute postprandial glucose in type 1 diabetes, and of prandial insulin dosing strategies for these factors.
- **Studies found:** fat n = 7, protein n = 7, GI n = 7; all showed that these factors change postprandial glycaemia. Ten studies looked at bolus dose and delivery pattern for high-fat and/or high-protein meals.
- **Fat:** the main effect was *late* postprandial hyperglycaemia; several studies showed *lower* glucose in the first 2-3 h, probably from slower gastric emptying.
- **Dosing:** study designs differed, so the best bolus delivery pattern was inconsistent across studies, but the studies agreed that high-fat/high-protein meals need more insulin than lower-fat/protein meals with the same carbohydrate.
- **Conclusion:** need for dosing algorithms based on whole meal composition rather than carbohydrate alone.

## Notes for Mellis

- Note: the caller's brief referred to "Bell et al., Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2015". The 2015 fat/protein/GI systematic review by Bell et al. is this Diabetes Care paper. (Bell's Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology paper was a 2014 meta-analysis of carbohydrate counting efficacy, which was not retrieved here.)
- Evidence level: systematic review of small, heterogeneous crossover studies. Strong on direction (more insulin, later), weak on exact split/dose.
