*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Lopez P, Smart C, Morbey C, McElduff P, Paterson M, King BR. Extended insulin boluses cannot control postprandial glycemia as well as a standard bolus in children and adults using insulin pump therapy. BMJ Open Diabetes Research & Care 2014;2(1):e000050, https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjdrc-2014-000050.*

## Key points

- 20 pump users aged 7-36 with type 1 diabetes ate the same moderate-GI breakfast (60 g carbohydrate, 10 g fat, 10 g protein) on six days, once with a standard bolus (SB) and five times with an extended (square-wave) bolus (EB) at different rates [Abstract; Methods].
- At 30, 60 and 90 minutes glucose rose less with the SB than with every EB (P < 0.05). At 60 minutes the excursion was 2.9 mmol/L after SB versus 4.7-6.4 mmol/L after the EBs [Abstract; Results].
- Even an EB delivering twice the usual dose over 2 hours (200% of the insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio dose) did not match the SB in the first 2 hours [Discussion].
- Peak rise and 2-hour area under the curve were lower with SB than with all EBs; hypoglycaemia was rare and similar (one event after SB, one after the fastest EB) [Results].
- Conclusion: for an ordinary mixed meal, extending the whole bolus leads to early post-meal hyperglycaemia; evidence for routine EB use is lacking [Discussion; Key messages].

## Bolus types and rates tested

- Pumps can give a standard (normal) bolus, an extended or square-wave bolus, or a combination/dual-wave bolus (part now, the rest spread out) [Introduction].
- All EBs ran for 2 hours. The rates mimic EBs set over 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6 hours: 100%, 50%, 33%, 25% and 16% of the carb-ratio dose per hour, so totals of about 200%, 100%, 66%, 50% and 32% of the usual dose within the 2-hour study [Methods].
- Boluses started just before eating, which the authors call common real-life practice [Study procedure].
- Hypoglycaemia was defined as BGL 3.5 mmol/L (63 mg/dL) or lower, or 4.0 mmol/L (72 mg/dL) or lower with symptoms; treated with 15 g oral carbohydrate [Study procedure].

## Results in numbers [Tables 1-3]

- Mean 2-hour excursion: SB 1.8; EB rates from fastest to slowest 3.6, 3.1, 5.4, 6.6 and 7.2 mmol/L.
- Peak excursion: SB 4.1 mmol/L versus 5.9-8.4 mmol/L with EBs; time to peak about 60 minutes with SB versus 71-95 minutes with EBs.
- SB versus the EB with the same total dose over 2 hours: SB was lower up to 60 minutes and at 90 minutes, but at 180 minutes glucose was lower after the EB (−3.3 vs −0.4 mmol/L from baseline) [Table 3].

## Interpretation by the authors

- Every meal type, including high-fat and low-GI meals, produces an early glucose rise that an EB alone does not cover; earlier pizza studies found the same early problem, although one showed better control at 4 hours with an EB [Discussion].
- An EB might still be useful in special cases such as gastroparesis, or where an early low is a risk (for example low-fat meals), and might work better if started 20-30 minutes before eating; these ideas were not tested [Discussion].
- The paper tests a pure extended bolus, not a dual-wave (combination) bolus with an upfront part [Introduction; Discussion].

## Limits of this summary

- Small study; one breakfast type with moderate fat and protein; only 2 hours of follow-up for most comparisons.
- Children and young adults with well-controlled diabetes and no complications; no AID system.
- It does not test high-fat/high-protein meals or combination boluses; read the original for clinical decisions.
