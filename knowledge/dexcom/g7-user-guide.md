*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Dexcom, Inc., Dexcom G7 CGM System User Guide (US English edition; chapters 2 Safety Information, 3 Screens, 4 Treatment Decisions and 11 Troubleshooting as held in our corpus), https://www.dexcom.com/.*

## Key points

- G7 is a real-time CGM for people with diabetes aged 2 years and older; it may replace fingersticks for treatment decisions and can feed automated insulin dosing (AID) systems [p. 5].
- Decisions should rest on trends and several readings in a row, not a single value [p. 5].
- Use a blood glucose (BG) meter instead of the sensor when there is no number or no trend arrow, when symptoms do not match the reading, and during the 30-minute warm-up [p. 6, 14, 41–42].
- "When in doubt, get your BG meter out": wash hands and check by fingerstick if how you feel does not fit the sensor value [p. 42].
- Do not stack insulin: after a meal bolus, a High alert 30 minutes later usually means the insulin has not acted yet; watch for the next 1–2 hours unless your clinician says otherwise [p. 42, 46].
- Trend arrows: falling arrow, consider a little less insulin than usual; rising arrow, consider a little more; agree the approach with your healthcare provider [p. 43].
- Hydroxyurea makes readings falsely high; do not use G7 for treatment decisions while taking it [p. 12, 15].
- Acetaminophen (paracetamol) up to 1 g (1,000 mg) every 6 hours does not prevent use of G7 readings; higher doses may give falsely high readings [p. 12, 15].
- After the sensor session there is a 12-hour grace period in which the system works as normal, to allow time to change the sensor [p. 21].
- No MRI or diathermy with any G7 part; CT is acceptable if the sensor is outside the scanned area and covered with a lead apron [p. 6, 125].

## When to use a fingerstick instead of the sensor (treatment decisions) [Chapter 4, p. 41–48]

- Two situations require the BG meter: (1) no sensor number and/or no arrow, which includes LOW (below 40 mg/dL, about 2.2 mmol/L), HIGH (above 400 mg/dL, about 22.2 mmol/L) and any System Alert such as Signal Loss or Brief Sensor Issue; (2) symptoms that do not match the reading [p. 31, 41–42].
- New users should keep using the meter for decisions until they know how G7 behaves for them; accuracy can differ between sensors, so compare symptoms and readings with each new sensor before relying on it [p. 15, 41].
- Worked examples in the guide (not medical advice): 70 mg/dL slowly falling, treat with G7 as usual; 132 mg/dL rising before breakfast, normal dose and consider a bit more; 108 mg/dL falling 60–90 mg/dL per 30 min before lunch, consider a bit less; 252 mg/dL with no arrow, use the meter; 123 mg/dL but shaky and sweaty, fingerstick first [p. 45–48].
- At security checkpoints (body scanner) or without a display device, use the meter for decisions [p. 11, 122].
- Followers' data (Dexcom Share/Follow) is always delayed and must not be used for treatment decisions [p. 13–14].

## Trend arrows [p. 32–33]

- Steady: change under 30 mg/dL (about 1.7 mmol/L) in 30 minutes.
- Slowly rising/falling: 30–60 mg/dL in 30 minutes.
- Rising/falling: 60–90 mg/dL in 30 minutes.
- Rapidly rising/falling: more than 90 mg/dL (about 5 mmol/L) in 30 minutes.
- No arrow: trend cannot be determined; use the meter.
- Arrows are meant to show where glucose may be within the next 30 minutes [p. 32].

## Warm-up, sensor session and grace period [p. 6, 10, 21–23]

- Warm-up takes about 30 minutes after insertion and pairing; there are no readings or alerts until it ends [p. 23].
- During the session a reading arrives every 5 minutes [p. 23].
- Grace period: an extra 12 hours after the session ends, with the system working as before [p. 21].
- Do not start a sensor after its Use By date; starting on or before it gives full wear [p. 11].
- Sensors can be stored between 36°F and 86°F (about 2–30°C), not frozen [p. 7].

## Accuracy, lag and calibration [Chapter 11, p. 106–108]

- Meter and sensor values differ partly because they measure different fluids: blood vs interstitial fluid; a lab result is the most accurate [p. 106].
- Other reasons for a difference: unwashed hands (use soap and water, not sanitiser), the first day of a new sensor (differences are usually larger and shrink over the first 24 hours), pressure on the sensor, fast-changing glucose (blood changes a little before interstitial fluid), and expired or badly stored strips [p. 106].
- If readings are consistently much higher or lower than the meter for several hours, consider calibrating [p. 107].
- Calibration is optional; it brings readings closer to the meter but may make them more or less accurate against the lab [p. 107].
- Calibration tips: do not calibrate when pressure is the cause; wait for stable glucose; use a fingertip sample; enter the value within 5 minutes; only values from 40 to 400 mg/dL; calibrate in the phone app or the receiver, not both; repeat if "Calibration Not Used" appears [p. 10, 107].

## Compression (pressure on the sensor) [p. 106–107]

- Lying on the sensor or other pressure on it can change readings; relieving the pressure should bring values back, and the sensor should recover by itself [p. 106–107].
- Avoid sites that get bumped, pushed or slept on [p. 11].

## Interfering substances [p. 12, 15]

- Hydroxyurea (used for some cancers and blood disorders): readings higher than real glucose, the size of the error depends on the amount in the body; risk of missed hypoglycaemia alerts and too much insulin. Do not use G7 for decisions; ask a physician about other monitoring [p. 12, 15].
- Acetaminophen: was a problem with G4/G5. With G7, a standard or maximum dose of 1 g every 6 hours still allows treatment decisions; more than that (e.g. > 1 g every 6 hours in adults) may make readings look falsely high [p. 12, 15].

## Imaging, travel and water [p. 6, 11, 122–125]

- MRI and diathermy: contraindicated (MR unsafe) [p. 6].
- CT, X-ray or radiation therapy: keep the sensor out of the scanned area and cover it with a lead apron; discuss with your provider [p. 6, 125].
- Airport: the sensor may be worn through walk-through metal detectors and body scanners, or ask for a pat-down; use the meter while without a display device [p. 11, 122].
- The sensor is waterproof to 8 feet (about 2.4 m), the receiver is not. Under water Bluetooth does not transmit; up to 24 hours of missed readings back-fill in the phone app or receiver (6 hours on the watch) [p. 125].

## Alerts and connection [p. 8–9, 12, 24, 114–120]

- Glucose alerts include Urgent Low, Urgent Low Soon, Low, High, Rising Fast and Falling Fast; alert levels should be agreed with the healthcare provider [p. 21–22].
- Urgent Low sounds even when the phone is muted unless Silence All is on; in Vibrate mode Urgent Low and technical alerts escalate to sound if not acknowledged [p. 8].
- Phone features such as Screen Time, Low Power Mode, Focus mode or battery savers can stop alerts; update the OS manually and check settings after updates [p. 8–9, 111].
- Keep the phone or receiver within 33 feet (about 10 m) of the sensor, the watch within 20 feet [p. 12].
- Signal Loss: banner after two missed readings, sound after about 20 minutes; use the meter until readings return [p. 117].
- Brief Sensor Issue: common on day 1, usually resolves within 3 hours; do not remove the sensor; use the meter meanwhile [p. 114].
- Sensor Failed: remove the sensor and start a new one; no readings until then [p. 115].

## Who should not rely on G7 and insertion sites [p. 5–7, 11]

- Not evaluated in people on dialysis or critically ill; readings may be inaccurate [p. 7].
- Wear on the back of the upper arm; children 2–6 years may also use the upper buttocks. Other sites may not work as expected [p. 7].
- Keep at least 3 inches (about 7.5 cm) from insulin infusion or injection sites; change site with each sensor [p. 11].
- Do not put sunscreen, repellent or lotion on the site [p. 10].
