ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Diabetes technologies: Insulin delivery. Pediatric Diabetes 2022;23:1406–1431, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13421.*

Section numbers below follow the chapter; page references were not used because the corpus copy is the early-view version with its own page numbers.

## Key points

- Offer young people the most advanced insulin delivery technology that is available, affordable and suitable for them (grade B). [Section 2.1]
- Pump therapy is recommended for youth of any age (A); it is safe and effective (A), reduces hypoglycaemia (B) and is linked to fewer chronic complications even at similar HbA1c (B). [Sections 2.3, 2.3.1]
- Infusion set failures are common and must be recognised quickly to avoid DKA (B). Some studies found a 2–5-fold higher DKA risk on pumps. [Section 2.3; Section 5.5]
- AID (closed loop) is strongly recommended (A); it raises time in range (TIR, 3.9–10 mmol/L / 70–180 mg/dL) by cutting both lows and highs (A), with the biggest gain overnight (A). Trials show about 10–15 percentage points more TIR and 0.3–0.7% lower HbA1c. [Section 2.3.5; Section 9.2]
- Most AID systems still need carbohydrate counting and a meal bolus; glucose will still swing, especially after meals; expect about one month of adjustment. [Section 2.4; Section 10.1.1]
- Mild hypoglycaemia: usually 10–15 g of fast carbohydrate, but often only 5–10 g on LGS, PLGS or AID systems. [Table 2; Section 8.2; Section 9.6]
- If someone chooses an open-source AID system, care providers are encouraged to support them (E), though clinicians should be cautious about recommending unapproved systems over commercial ones. [Section 2.3.5; Section 9.4.2]
- Persistent high glucose on any pump: check ketones, change the infusion set and give insulin by pen or syringe. [Section 10.1.2]

## Pump therapy and starting settings

Pumps are recommended for all youth, and specifically for recurrent severe hypoglycaemia, wide glucose swings, HbA1c above 7.0% or TIR below 70%, very young children and infants, marked dawn phenomenon, needle phobia, ketosis-prone people and competitive athletes. The only listed contraindications are the person's own wish not to use technology and severe skin problems with wear. ISPAD's preschool chapter names the pump as the preferred mode below 7 years. [Table 1; Section 5.2]

Registry data link pumps with lower HbA1c (8.0% vs 8.5% in a pooled 55,000-child analysis), lower insulin doses, and fewer severe hypoglycaemia and DKA events; observational reductions in HbA1c ranged from 0.2% to 1.1%. Starting a pump at diagnosis works, and those with the highest HbA1c (>9%) improve most. [Sections 5.1–5.2]

Initial settings come from the total daily dose (TDD) before the switch [Table 2; Section 5.6.3]:

- **TDD:** consider reducing it at pump start in people already at target or with frequent or severe hypoglycaemia.
- **Basal/bolus split:** about 50/50 in older children and teens; basal may be only 30–35% of TDD under 7 years.
- **Basal rate:** basal share of TDD ÷ 24 (example: 20 U basal → 0.8 U/h). Preschoolers may need more basal between 9 pm and midnight and less before breakfast; teenagers may need more in the early morning (dawn phenomenon).
- **Correction factor / ISF:** keep existing factors if already used; otherwise 1800 ÷ TDD (mg/dL) or 100 ÷ TDD (mmol/L), raised to 2000 ÷ TDD for insulin-sensitive people or lowered to 1500 ÷ TDD for insulin-resistant people.
- **Insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio:** keep existing ratios, or 500 ÷ TDD; young children may need stronger meal coverage with a 350 rule.
- **Follow-up:** judge doses on pre-meal and 2-hour post-meal sensor values and overnight traces (with fingersticks: pre- and 2 h post-meal, plus midnight and 3 am checks).
- **Good pump habits:** bolus for carbohydrates, ideally before eating; treat hypoglycaemia with 10–15 g fast carbohydrate (5–10 g on LGS, PLGS or AID); change the infusion set at least every 3 days; wear CGM continuously for systems that use it.

More pre-programmed basal rates, more boluses per day and basal below 50% of TDD are associated with better HbA1c. Meal announcement should be reinforced at every visit. Diluted insulin can help children with very small doses. [Section 5.6.3]

## Bolus calculator, insulin on board and extended/combo bolus

- **Temporary basal rates** adjust for day-to-day sensitivity changes: lower for physical activity, higher for illness. Temporary reduction or full suspension of basal can help prevent exercise hypoglycaemia. Separate basal patterns can be set for predictable changes such as menstruation. [Section 5.6.4]
- **Bolus types:** normal (all at once), extended or square (spread over a chosen time), and combo or dual wave (part now, rest extended). High-fat meals may be best covered with extended or combo boluses because fat delays the glucose rise; the chapter's example is 40% upfront and 60% over 4 hours. [Section 5.6.4]
- **Insulin on board:** pumps can reduce a new bolus by the insulin still active from the last one, which may lower the risk of severe hypoglycaemia after boluses. Connected pens with calculators do the same and may prevent hypoglycaemia from stacked correction doses given too often. [Section 5.6.4; Section 4.1]
- **Calculator set-up** needs correction factor, target glucose, duration of insulin action and carbohydrate ratios, optionally different by time of day. [Section 4.1]
- On AID, insulin added automatically by the algorithm counts as insulin on board and is subtracted by the bolus calculator. Overriding the calculator to give more than suggested can leave a lot of insulin on board. [Section 10.1.2]

## Infusion set failure, DKA risk and ketones

- 40–68% of pump users have adverse events such as set failures, pump faults or alarm problems. The main danger is a blocked or dislodged cannula that stops insulin and leads toward ketoacidosis. Steel cannulas kink or dislodge less and may suit the youngest children. [Section 5.5]
- Education on DKA risk and on managing persistent hyperglycaemia is the cornerstone of prevention. Mild DKA can often be reversed quickly with extra insulin by syringe or pen as soon as high glucose and ketones appear. Some have tried adding a small dose of long-acting insulin to pump therapy. [Section 5.5]
- Users of systems that can stop insulin (LGS, PLGS) do not need daily ketone checks; ketones should be checked when glucose stays high or during illness, as for any pump user. [Section 8.1]
- In a study of planned 2-hour overnight suspensions (only when bedtime glucose was <16.7 mmol/L / 300 mg/dL and β-hydroxybutyrate <0.5 mmol/L), morning glucose was only about 2.8 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) higher and ketones not significantly higher. [Section 7.1]
- Lipohypertrophy changes absorption; rotate sites and avoid affected areas for months. A CGM sensor can still be placed in lipohypertrophic skin without losing accuracy. [Section 5.5]

## Sensor-augmented pump, LGS and PLGS

- **SAP** beats MDI with fingersticks for HbA1c without more severe hypoglycaemia (A), but sensor use must be at least 60% of the time (A). Each 10% more sensor wear adds about 1.1% TIR and removes about 1.0% time above 10 mmol/L (180 mg/dL). [Section 2.3.2; Section 6.1]
- **LGS** stops insulin when sensor glucose reaches a set low threshold, for up to 2 hours unless the user restarts it; it reduces the depth and length of lows without raising HbA1c (A). [Section 2.3.3; Section 7.1]
- **PLGS** stops basal insulin when a low is predicted (A). Examples: Medtronic 640G–780G suspend when glucose is predicted to come within 1.1 mmol/L (20 mg/dL) of the low limit in 30 minutes, for 30–120 minutes; Tandem Basal-IQ uses a fixed 4.4 mmol/L (80 mg/dL) and resumes once glucose rises (31% less time <3.9 mmol/L / 70 mg/dL). Morning ketosis (BHB >0.6 mmol/L) was not more common with PLGS. [Section 8.1]
- Where AID is unavailable, PLGS is strongly recommended; where only LGS exists, LGS is strongly recommended (A). [Section 2.3.4]
- Practical: set alerts that lead to action (for example a low alert so fast carbohydrate can be taken). With LGS, let the system act at night; during the day eat carbohydrate and resume basal after an alert. If a low happens despite a PLGS suspension, a smaller treatment (5–10 g) may prevent rebound highs. [Section 8.2]

## AID / hybrid closed-loop behaviour

- Components: pump, CGM and an algorithm (PID, model predictive control or fuzzy logic; no algorithm has been shown best). [Section 9.1]
- Fully closed loop without meal input produced large post-meal rises, so **hybrid closed loop (HCL)** requires manual meal boluses and automates basal; **advanced HCL (AHCL)** also gives automatic correction boluses. The useful split is manual vs automated insulin rather than basal vs bolus. [Section 9.1]
- Targets are either a single treat-to-target value (for example 5.8 mmol/L / 105 mg/dL) or a treat-to-range band (for example 6.2–8.9 mmol/L / 112–160 mg/dL). [Section 9.1]
- Benefits hold in very young children (2–5 years), children 6–13, adolescents and young adults (Table 3). Those with the highest HbA1c/GMI gain most in time above range, while those with low HbA1c gain in time below range; access should not be restricted. [Section 9.2–9.3; Table 3]
- Time out of automation ("system-mandated exits", mostly when sensor data are missing) frustrates users and drives discontinuation; newer systems with factory-calibrated sensors have fewer exits. [Section 9.3]
- In some systems delivery parameters are tied to TDD and adapt as needs change; carbohydrate ratios usually need adjusting after the switch. [Section 10.1.1]
- The CARES framework (how it Calculates, what can be Adjusted, when it Reverts to open loop, Education/expectations, Sensor and Share) helps compare systems. Reasons to choose open loop manually include ketosis and steroid use. [Section 9.6; Table 4]

## Meals, exercise and other limitations on AID

- Post-meal hyperglycaemia remains common. Ultra-rapid insulin has not shown clear benefit in short AID trials; intraperitoneal and inhaled insulin, adjunct drugs (pramlintide, GLP-1 analogues, SGLT inhibitors) and dual-hormone systems are under study. [Section 9.5]
- Exercise is still a problem; approaches tested include dual-hormone systems, lower pre-exercise meal boluses, a snack just before exercise, and heart-rate signals. Users should be taught how to manage exercise (see ISPAD exercise chapter). [Section 9.5; Section 9.6]
- Use AID "as intended": announce only carbohydrates that will really be eaten, follow the calculator, avoid "tricking" the system, and talk to the team about patterns that may need changes in ratio or correction factor, or earlier bolusing. [Section 10.1.2]
- Mild hypoglycaemia on AID often needs only 5–10 g carbohydrate; treatment may need to be smaller after long basal suspensions on other devices. [Section 9.6]

## Open-source AID and diluted insulin

- Open-source ("DIY") AID is used by several thousand people. In silico work suggested relative safety with over- and under-estimated or late meal boluses; a real-world study of 558 users (over half under 25) showed better TIR and fewer severe lows; an RCT in ages 7–70 found about 14% adjusted TIR difference versus CGM alone. Because these systems lack regulatory approval, clinicians should be cautious about recommending them over approved systems, but should support people who choose them (E). [Section 9.4.2]
- Diluted insulin in AID for children aged 1–7 brought no benefit over U100 in a 3-week trial; that trial also showed very young children have the largest day-to-day variation in insulin needs, which supports early AID. [Section 9.4.1]

## Education and expectations

- Use a standardised training approach (C), with follow-up within the first month, because most people who stop HCL/AID do so in the first 1–3 months; up to 30% of youth may discontinue AID. [Section 2.4; Section 10]
- Training covers device basics, CGM use (alarm fatigue, skin, consistent wear) and troubleshooting of high glucose and set failure. [Section 10.1.2]
- Set realistic expectations (B/C): glucose will improve but not always reach target; night-time improves most; meals still need boluses; allow at least a month to adapt. [Section 2.4; Section 10.1.1]
- Do not make families "earn" devices by reaching an HbA1c first; refer for psychological support when needed. [Section 10.1]
- With better AID, a tighter range of 3.9–7.8 mmol/L (70–140 mg/dL) may become the target in future. [Section 11]

## Limits of this summary

- Written for children, adolescents and young adults with type 1 diabetes; device examples are the commercial systems available in 2022, and features differ between systems.
- Grades range from A to E; many practical points (starting rules, carbohydrate amounts on AID) are expert guidance.
- The trial details in Table 3, connected-pen evidence and the psychosocial literature are summarised only briefly. The 2024 ISPAD update (separate file) revises parts of this chapter.
- Read the original and follow the care team's advice for any clinical decision.
