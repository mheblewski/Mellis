*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Fiasp 100 units/mL (insulin aspart, fast-acting formulation), Summary of Product Characteristics, Annex I of the EPAR product information, European Medicines Agency / Novo Nordisk A/S (first authorised 9 January 2017; version retrieved 2026-10-16), https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/medicines/human/EPAR/fiasp.*

## Key points

- Fiasp is insulin aspart with added nicotinamide (vitamin B3), which speeds up the first part of absorption compared with NovoRapid (standard insulin aspart) [Section 5.1, 5.2].
- Onset of action: about 5 minutes earlier than NovoRapid; time to maximum glucose infusion rate about 11 minutes earlier [Section 5.1].
- Peak (maximum glucose-lowering effect): between 1 and 3 hours after injection [Section 5.1].
- Duration of action: 3–5 hours, shorter than NovoRapid; it varies with dose, injection site, blood flow, temperature and physical activity [Section 4.2, 5.1].
- Insulin is detectable in the blood about 4 minutes after a subcutaneous injection; in the first 15 minutes about four times, and in the first 30 minutes about twice, as much insulin is available as with NovoRapid [Section 5.2].
- In a pump (CSII), time to maximum concentration is 26 minutes shorter than NovoRapid, with about three times as much insulin in the first 30 minutes [Section 5.2].
- Timing: give it 0–2 minutes before the start of a meal; it may also be given up to 20 minutes after starting to eat (in children: when it is uncertain how much will be eaten) [Section 4.2].
- Hypoglycaemia may come earlier after a dose than with other mealtime insulins, because the action starts earlier [Section 4.4, 4.8].
- Approved for adults, adolescents and children from 1 year of age; no clinical experience below 2 years [Section 4.1, 4.2].
- Pump use: vial insulin may stay in a pump reservoir for at most 6 days; PumpCart cartridges are only for pumps designed for them (Accu-Chek Insight, YpsoPump) [Section 6.3, 6.6].

## Pharmacodynamics: onset, peak and duration of action [Section 5.1]

- Mechanism: like all insulins it binds to the insulin receptor, increases glucose uptake into muscle and fat, and reduces glucose output from the liver; it also inhibits lipolysis [Section 5.1].
- Clamp data: glucose-lowering effect in the first 30 minutes (AUC GIR 0–30 min) was 51 mg/kg with Fiasp vs 29 mg/kg with NovoRapid (ratio 1.74). Total and maximum glucose-lowering effect were similar to NovoRapid and rise linearly with dose [Section 5.1].
- The SmPC stresses that the greater early effect must be taken into account when prescribing [Section 4.2, 5.1].
- Day-to-day variability within the same patient was low (CV about 26% for the first hour, about 18% for total effect) [Section 5.1].

## Pharmacokinetics [Section 5.2]

- Mean Cmax after 0.2 units/kg: 298 pmol/L, similar to NovoRapid; total exposure similar [Section 5.2].
- Subcutaneous half-life: 57 minutes (similar to NovoRapid); after intravenous use the half-life is about 10 minutes [Section 5.2].
- Bioavailability about 80% in abdomen, upper arm and thigh. The fast onset holds at all sites, but early exposure is lower in the thigh than in the abdomen or upper arm [Section 5.2].
- Higher BMI slows initial absorption (less so than with NovoRapid) [Section 5.2].
- Elderly: total exposure and Cmax about 30% higher than in younger adults [Section 5.2].
- Children (6–11 years) and adolescents (12–18 years): onset and early exposure similar to adults; total exposure lower at 0.2 units/kg, Cmax similar [Section 5.2].

## Timing of the mealtime dose (bolus timing) [Section 4.2, 4.4, 5.1]

- Standard: subcutaneous dose up to 2 minutes before the meal starts; option of dosing up to 20 minutes after the start of the meal [Section 4.2].
- When food absorption may be delayed (other illness or medicines), the early onset must be considered [Section 4.4].
- Trial data, type 1 diabetes, 26 weeks (with insulin detemir): mealtime Fiasp lowered HbA1c slightly more than mealtime NovoRapid (difference −0.15%); post-meal Fiasp (20 min after start) was non-inferior to mealtime NovoRapid. Mealtime Fiasp gave lower 1-hour and 2-hour post-meal glucose rises; post-meal Fiasp gave a higher 1-hour rise and a similar 2-hour rise [Section 5.1, Table 3].
- In that trial, rates of severe or BG-confirmed hypoglycaemia (plasma glucose < 3.1 mmol/L, about 56 mg/dL) were similar across arms [Section 5.1, Table 3].
- Missed mealtime dose: check glucose to decide whether a dose is needed, then go back to the usual schedule at the next meal [Section 4.2].

## Dosing and dose adjustment [Section 4.2]

- Dosing is individual. Total daily insulin need is usually 0.5–1 unit/kg/day; in a basal-bolus regimen roughly half may be Fiasp [Section 4.2].
- Insulin-naïve type 1 diabetes: starting total daily dose of 0.2–0.4 units/kg as a general rule, about half as mealtime insulin split by meal size and composition [Section 4.2].
- Doses may need changing with more physical activity, a change in diet or other illness, with closer glucose checks at those times [Section 4.2].
- Switching from another mealtime insulin can be unit-for-unit, with close glucose monitoring in the first weeks [Section 4.2].
- Type 2 diabetes titration table (by self-measured glucose before the next meal): < 4 mmol/L (< 71 mg/dL) −1 unit; 4–6 mmol/L (71–108 mg/dL) no change; > 6 mmol/L (> 108 mg/dL) +1 unit [Section 4.2, Table 1].
- Renal or hepatic impairment may reduce insulin needs; monitor more closely [Section 4.2].

## Pump use (CSII) [Section 4.2, 4.4, 6.3, 6.6]

- Vial insulin can be used in pumps suitable for insulin infusion; it then covers both bolus (about 50%) and basal needs. Preferably in the abdomen; do not dilute or mix with other insulins in a pump [Section 4.2].
- Change tubing and cannula as the infusion set instructions say; rotate infusion sites within a region [Section 4.2].
- Pump users must be trained to inject insulin and have backup insulin ready in case the pump fails [Section 4.2].
- Pump or infusion set failure can quickly lead to hyperglycaemia and ketosis; the cause must be found and corrected promptly, and injections may be needed in the meantime [Section 4.4].
- Maximum time in a pump: 6 days for insulin drawn from a vial; an opened vial lasts at most 4 weeks below 30°C including time in the reservoir [Section 6.3, 6.6].
- PumpCart: 2 weeks below 30°C, then up to 7 days below 37°C inside a compatible pump; only for pumps designed for it; using it in another device may cause wrong dosing [Section 4.4, 6.3, 6.6].
- Pump trial: no confirmed infusion set occlusions over 6 weeks; infusion site reactions 10.0% vs 8.3% for comparator in pump users [Section 4.8, 5.1].

## Hypoglycaemia [Section 4.4, 4.8, 4.9]

- Causes named: missed meal, unplanned strenuous exercise, a dose too high for the need [Section 4.4].
- Warning symptoms can change when control improves, and may disappear in long-standing diabetes [Section 4.4].
- Symptoms listed include cold sweat, pale skin, tremor, anxiety, confusion, poor concentration, drowsiness, strong hunger, vision changes, headache, nausea and palpitations [Section 4.8].
- Treatment: mild episodes with oral glucose or sugary products (patients should always carry some). Severe episodes (patient cannot self-treat): glucagon 0.5–1 mg intramuscularly or subcutaneously by a trained person, or intravenous glucose by a professional; give IV glucose if there is no response to glucagon within 10–15 minutes; after waking, give oral carbohydrate to prevent a relapse [Section 4.9].
- Driving: hypoglycaemia can impair concentration; take care, especially with reduced hypoglycaemia awareness [Section 4.7].
- Beta-blockers can mask hypoglycaemia symptoms; alcohol can increase or reduce insulin's effect [Section 4.5].

## Hyperglycaemia, DKA and illness [Section 4.4, 4.5]

- Too little insulin or stopping treatment can cause hyperglycaemia and diabetic ketoacidosis, which can be life-threatening [Section 4.4].
- Illness, particularly infections and fever, usually raises insulin needs [Section 4.4].
- Medicines that may raise insulin needs include glucocorticoids, thyroid hormones, oral contraceptives, thiazides, sympathomimetics and growth hormone; medicines that may lower them include other glucose-lowering drugs, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, salicylates and GLP-1 receptor agonists [Section 4.5].

## Children and adolescents [Section 4.2, 4.4, 4.8, 5.1]

- Use from age 1 year; no clinical experience under 2 years [Section 4.2].
- Recommended 0–2 minutes before the meal, with the option of up to 20 minutes after the start when intake is uncertain [Section 4.2].
- If given after the start of the last meal of the day, glucose should be watched closely to avoid night-time hypoglycaemia [Section 4.4].
- Paediatric trial (1–18 years, n = 777, 26 weeks, with insulin degludec): mealtime Fiasp gave better HbA1c than NovoRapid (−0.17%); post-meal Fiasp was non-inferior; no overall rise in severe or confirmed hypoglycaemia [Section 5.1].
- Lipodystrophy was reported more often in children (2.1% vs 1.6% NovoRapid) than in adult trials [Section 4.8].

## Injection sites and other cautions [Section 4.2, 4.4]

- Injection: abdominal wall or upper arm; rotate sites within a region to reduce lipodystrophy and cutaneous amyloidosis [Section 4.2].
- Injecting into lumpy (lipodystrophic) areas can delay absorption; switching suddenly to healthy tissue has caused hypoglycaemia, so check glucose and consider dose changes after such a switch [Section 4.4].
- Do not use insulin that is not clear and colourless or that has been frozen [Section 6.6].

## Limits of this summary

- Covers the SmPC (Annex I) only; labelling and the package leaflet are left out, as are most trial tables and type 2 diabetes details.
- Trial data are in adults with type 1 and type 2 diabetes and in children 1–18 years; experience under 2 years and over 75 years is limited.
- Pharmacokinetic numbers come from clamp and pharmacokinetic studies; real-life action varies with dose, site, temperature and activity.
- For prescribing or dosing decisions read the current EMA product information and follow the treating team.
