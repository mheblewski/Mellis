*Our summary (Mellis knowledge base), written in our own words. It is not the original text and not medical advice. Original: Adolfsson P, Taplin CE, Zaharieva DP, et al. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Exercise in children and adolescents with diabetes. Pediatric Diabetes 2022;23:1341–1372, doi 10.1111/pedi.13452, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13452.*

Page numbers are journal pages (1341–1372). Letters A–E are the evidence grades given in the chapter. The chapter stresses that most numbers come from small studies in mostly male adults, and that every plan is a starting point to be adjusted by trial and error.

## Key points

- Hypoglycaemia risk is raised during exercise, soon after it and for up to 24 h afterwards, because insulin sensitivity rises (A). [Executive summary, p. 1342]
- Aim to keep glucose in an exercise range of 5.0–15.0 mmol/L (90–270 mg/dL) by combining insulin and food strategies (A). [p. 1342; Section 7.1, p. 1351]
- Severe hypoglycaemia in the previous 24 h is generally a contraindication to exercise (A). [p. 1342]
- Ketones: exercise is contraindicated with blood ketones ≥1.5 mmol/L (urine 2+ or 4.0 mmol/L). With blood ketones 0.6–1.4 mmol/L, postpone exercise until the cause is checked and give half the usual correction dose (or 0.05 U/kg) (B). [p. 1342]
- CGM is the preferred monitoring during exercise (A). CGM lags during long aerobic exercise (about 12 ± 11 min), so confirm with a fingerstick when a low is recent, present or impending (A). [p. 1342; Section 7.3, p. 1355]
- Carbohydrate during exercise: about 0.3–0.5 g/kg/h when only basal insulin is active (more than 2 h after the last meal bolus), about 0.5–1.0 g/kg/h with high circulating bolus insulin. [Section 4.3, p. 1345; Section 7.3, p. 1353]
- Meal bolus before exercise (meal within about 2 h): starting plan −50% for aerobic and mixed exercise, −25% for anaerobic (pump and MDI); on hybrid closed loop the starting plan is −25%. [Tables 2–3, p. 1350–1351; Section 8.3.1, p. 1359]
- After exercise in the afternoon or evening (after 16:00, over 30 min): reduce overnight basal by 20% (pump: for 6 h; MDI: evening basal), and/or a bedtime snack of 0.4 g/kg low-GI carbohydrate without bolus if glucose is under 10.0 mmol/L (180 mg/dL), adding 15 g protein if under 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL). [Tables 2–3; Section 7.5, p. 1357–1358]
- AID / hybrid closed loop: set the exercise or temporary target 1–2 h (studies: 90–120 min) before aerobic exercise; avoid pump suspension longer than about 90–120 min. [Section 8.2, p. 1358–1359; Section 8.7, p. 1360]
- Moderate aerobic activity such as 15–45 min of walking or cycling between meals safely lowers glucose above 10.5 mmol/L (190 mg/dL) (B). [p. 1342; Section 7.7, p. 1358]
- Alcohol should be avoided before and during exercise (A). [p. 1342; Section 6.3, p. 1349]

## Types of exercise: aerobic, anaerobic, mixed

[Section 5, Table 1, p. 1346–1348]
- **Aerobic** (walking, running, cycling, swimming, rowing at moderate intensity): glucose usually falls, because muscle takes up more glucose than the liver releases. Duration adds to the risk; even low-intensity long exercise needs insulin and food changes that grow as the activity goes on. [Section 4.2, p. 1344–1345]
- **Mixed with short bursts** (football, basketball, handball, martial arts): glucose falls less, or stays stable. Such interval-type activity may not need insulin changes.
- **Mixed with longer bursts** (resistance or circuit training, gymnastics, sprint training): glucose may stay stable or rise.
- **Anaerobic / very high intensity** (sprints, weightlifting, short races): glucose can rise, especially early in the day with little bolus insulin on board and when done alone, and with competition stress. Insulin reductions are often not advised; a post-exercise correction may be considered (B). [Section 5.2, p. 1347–1348]
- What drives the drop: circulating insulin level, intensity and duration; also counter-regulatory hormones, starting glucose, fitness, age, sex. Exercise speeds up absorption of subcutaneous insulin, so inject or place sites away from working muscles (B). Hypoglycaemia in the previous 24–48 h, obesity and cold may blunt counter-regulation. [Sections 5.4–5.5, p. 1348]

## Insulin on board and timing

[Section 4.3, p. 1345]
- Rapid-acting analogues peak about 60–100 min after injection and act for up to 5 h. Exercise is easiest to manage with little or no active bolus insulin, which is rare in children who eat often.
- When exercise is within 2–3 h of a meal, consider reducing that meal's bolus. Large reductions given more than 90 min before exercise may cause hyperglycaemia before the start.
- Afternoon exercise (low or high intensity) raises the risk of delayed nocturnal hypoglycaemia, often 7–11 h later; this applies even when high-intensity exercise caused a high at the time. Morning exercise is one way to lower the night-time risk.

## Before planned exercise: bolus and basal reductions (pump and MDI)

Based on Tables 2 (pump) and 3 (MDI) and Section 7.2 [p. 1350–1352]. Applies to activity of at least 30 min. "Starting plan" is the first suggestion (evidence level D); adjust only the step that caused a high or low.

Meal bolus if the meal is within about 2 h of exercise (if the meal is more than 2 h before, give the normal dose to avoid hyperglycaemia):
- Aerobic: start −50%; if glucose went above 15.0 mmol/L (270 mg/dL), use −25%; if it went under 5.0 mmol/L (90 mg/dL), use −75%.
- Mixed: start −50%; high → −25%; low → −75%.
- Anaerobic: start −25%; high → normal dose; low → −50%.

Pump basal (when exercise is more than 120 min after the last meal bolus), set 90 min before:
- Aerobic: start −50%; high → −25%; low → −80%.
- Mixed: start −25%; high → normal; low → −50%.
- Anaerobic: start normal; high → normal plus a small bolus 15 min before; low → −25%.
- Basal cuts of 50% or 80% set 90 min before reduced hypoglycaemia; disconnecting the pump only at the start usually does not prevent it. [Section 7.2, p. 1352]

Pre-exercise meal [Section 7.2, p. 1352]:
- Within 2 h of exercise: a low-fat, carbohydrate-rich meal of 1.0–1.5 g/kg; aim for the meal within about 60–90 min of the start when the bolus is reduced.
- 2–3 h before: keep meal carbohydrate at most 2 g/kg to limit insulin on board.
- More than 3 h before: 1–3 g/kg carbohydrate, moderate to low fat (endurance athletes up to 4 g/kg).
- Body-weight rule: if BMI centile is ≥91st, use ideal body weight (BMI at the 50th centile for age × height²), unless high BMI is muscle. Consider less carbohydrate for people with less lean mass (females, sedentary).

## During exercise: carbohydrate amounts (g/kg) and glucose thresholds

General [Section 7.3, p. 1353; p. 1356]:
- Extra carbohydrate is the main tool during activity: 0.5–1.0 g/kg/h with high bolus insulin, 0.3–0.5 g/kg/h if more than 2 h since the last meal bolus.
- Gut absorption tops out around 1.0–1.2 g/min, so the tables cap body weight at 60 kg when calculating.
- With frequent checks (every 20 min), fast high-GI sources (dextrose tablets, glucose drinks, gels) work best; slower foods (fruit, biscuits, chocolate) taken every 20 min raise the risk of a low during and a high after. With infrequent checks, slower carbohydrates may avoid an early high. Sports drinks with 8–10% carbohydrate work in adolescents.

Fingerstick table, amounts before and every 30 min (Table 4, level D) [p. 1352]:
- Above 15.0 mmol/L (270 mg/dL) with ketones: see the ketone rules below. Above 15.0 with ketones under 0.6 mmol/L: consider half the usual correction.
- 10.1–15.0 mmol/L (181–270 mg/dL): no carbohydrate.
- 7.0–10.0 mmol/L (126–180 mg/dL), the exercise target: 0.2–0.5 g/kg if glucose is expected to fall; none if stable or rising.
- 5.0–6.9 mmol/L (90–125 mg/dL): 0.5 g/kg if falling; 0.2 g/kg if stable or rising.
- 4.0–4.9 mmol/L (70–89 mg/dL): 0.3 g/kg and delay or stop exercise for 20 min.
- 3.0–3.9 mmol/L (54–70 mg/dL): treat hypoglycaemia and wait until above 4.9 mmol/L (89 mg/dL).
- Under 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL): treat and do not start exercise (counter-regulation is impaired).
- If hypoglycaemia risk or unawareness is medium or high, raise the exercise target to 8.0–11.0 mmol/L (145–198 mg/dL) or 9.0–12.0 mmol/L (162–216 mg/dL).

CGM table, amounts before and every 20 min by value and trend arrow (Table 5, level D) [p. 1353]:
- Same glucose bands as above; amounts grow as the arrow points further down. Examples for "expected to fall": at 7.0–10.0 mmol/L, 0 g/kg with a rising arrow, 0.2 with a flat arrow, 0.4 with a fast-falling arrow; at 5.0–6.9 mmol/L, 0.1 (rising fast) up to 0.5 (falling fast); at 4.0–4.9 mmol/L, 0.2 up to 0.5 g/kg, and delay or stop exercise for 20 min. At 10.1–15.0 mmol/L only falling arrows get 0.1–0.2 g/kg. "Stable or rising" columns use smaller amounts.
- For fast-falling arrows at low levels, confirm with a fingerstick because CGM may lag.
- Smaller amounts every 10–20 min guided by CGM have been shown to remove clinically important hypoglycaemia (<3.0 mmol/L, <54 mg/dL). [Section 7.3, p. 1355]

## CGM accuracy and trend arrows during exercise

[Section 7.3, Tables 7–8, p. 1355–1356]
- During exercise, MARD rises (from about 10% to 13.6%) and the lag between blood and sensor glucose grows from about 5 min to 12–24 min; the faster glucose moves, the longer the lag.
- Check a fingerstick when sensor glucose is borderline.
- Set the low alert higher during exercise, for example 5.6 mmol/L (100 mg/dL). Do not restart exercise once sensor glucose has gone under 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL). Use sensor glucose and trend after exercise to decide on carbohydrate; encourage remote followers during, after exercise and overnight.
- Trend arrows differ by brand (Table 7). For Dexcom, one arrow down means a fall of 30–45 mg/dL (1.7–2.5 mmol/L) in 15 min, two arrows down more than 45 mg/dL (2.5 mmol/L), a diagonal arrow 15–30 mg/dL (0.8–1.7 mmol/L), flat under 15 mg/dL (0.8 mmol/L).
- Heat, dehydration, cold and altitude can affect sensor or meter accuracy. [Section 9.4, p. 1362]

## Ketones and exercise

- Measure ketones before exercise, ideally in blood, when glucose suggests insulin deficiency (D). [p. 1342]
- Blood ketones ≥1.5 mmol/L: do not start or continue exercise; follow usual ketone advice. [p. 1342; Section 10.1, p. 1363; Tables 4–5]
- Tables 4–5 (glucose above 15.0 mmol/L / 270 mg/dL): ketones 1.1–1.4 mmol/L → half correction by pen, recheck after 60 min; ketones 0.6–1.0 mmol/L → half correction by pen and wait 15 min before exercising.
- Executive summary wording: with 0.6–1.4 mmol/L, postpone exercise until the cause is evaluated and give half the usual correction (or 0.05 U/kg) (B). Section 10.1 says ketones 1.0–1.4 mmol/L (urine ++) mean postponing until ketones normalise after a correction bolus. The chapter is not fully consistent on the exact bands.
- Hyperglycaemia ≥15.0 mmol/L (≥270 mg/dL) with ketones caused by insulin deficiency (not by excess carbohydrate) is a temporary contraindication (D). [p. 1343; Section 10.1, p. 1363]
- Above 15.0 mmol/L with ketones under 1.5 mmol/L, half the usual correction can bring glucose down. [p. 1356]
- Long pump suspension (over 120 min), especially in children aged 4–9, may raise ketones and risks forgetting to restart. [Section 9.3, p. 1362]

## After exercise: post-exercise meal, rebound highs

[Section 7.4, p. 1356]
- Post-exercise meal bolus: starting plan −50% after aerobic, −25% after mixed or anaerobic (pump and MDI tables); on hybrid closed loop −25% for any exercise type (expert opinion). [Tables 2–3; Section 8.3.2, p. 1359]
- A balanced meal within about 90 min after exercise, 1–4 g/kg carbohydrate (3 g/kg or more only for endurance athletes) with 15–20 g protein, uses the 2-h recovery window.
- Reasons for a rise right after exercise: lactate and adrenaline after anaerobic work; insulin missing after pump disconnection (one option: bolus 50% of the missed basal before or during activity); too much or too slow carbohydrate during exercise (more than 1.0 g/min). A 10–15 min cool-down and a 50% correction are common suggestions; in one study, 100% and 150% corrections after high-intensity interval training worked better than 50% without significantly more hypoglycaemia.

## Delayed and nocturnal hypoglycaemia

[Section 4.3, p. 1345; Section 7.5, p. 1357–1358]
- After about 45 min of exercise the hypoglycaemia risk lasts 7–11 h, so activity after 4 p.m. increases night-time risk.
- Pump: basal −20% for 6 h overnight reduced lows in youth (−40% if lows still occur). MDI: evening basal −20% (−40% if needed). A closed-loop study also saw an average 20% overnight cut after exercise.
- Bedtime snack: only if glucose before bed is under 10.0 mmol/L (180 mg/dL), 0.4 g/kg low- to medium-GI carbohydrate without bolus (0.2 g/kg if this led to highs, 0.6 g/kg if lows persisted); add 15 g protein if under 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL). Younger children, and those with overweight, almost certainly need smaller snacks.
- About 45 min of exercise at midday does not have the same overnight effect, so school sports or lunchtime activity usually do not need a basal change.

## Hybrid closed loop (AID): exercise targets, temporary targets, exercise modes

[Section 8, Table 9, p. 1357–1361]
- All commercial HCL systems have an exercise or activity target that raises the glucose target and reduces insulin delivery. Table 9 lists: MiniMed 670G/770G 8.3 mmol/L (150 mg/dL) temp target; MiniMed 780G 8.3 mmol/L (150 mg/dL); Control-IQ exercise activity 7.8–8.9 mmol/L (140–160 mg/dL); Omnipod 5 activity 8.3 mmol/L (150 mg/dL); CamAPS FX customisable "Ease-off"; DIY systems (OpenAPS, AndroidAPS, Loop) with temporary targets, profile switches, overrides or activity modes that can be timed or scheduled.
- Set the exercise target well before aerobic exercise; studies used 90–120 min before sessions of 40+ min. Setting it later still helps: it stops automatic correction boluses (on systems that have them) and lowers basal during the activity. Keep a higher target for 1–2 h in recovery if hypoglycaemia risk is raised. Short (<30 min), some high-intensity, or fasted sessions may not need one.
- Bolus before exercise: meal bolus action can extend into exercise within 1–3 h of a meal. In adults, an exercise target plus a 33% meal-bolus cut gave the least time below 3.9 mmol/L. The chapter's starting plan for aerobic and mixed exercise soon after a meal is −25%; for high-intensity or competition, possibly no reduction. Where no bolus-reduction function exists, entering fewer carbohydrates, or a separate activity profile with higher ISF and weaker ICR, are options, but these have not been studied and need care.
- Carbohydrate before exercise: snacks taken 20 min or more before tend to raise glucose and trigger extra automated insulin, increasing the risk of a low during exercise. Consensus: take pre-exercise carbohydrate within 5–10 min of the start (or if a low develops). If food is needed 1–2 h before, bolus about 25% less and turn on activity mode.
- During exercise: use CGM value and arrows; smaller, frequent amounts reduce rebound highs; for unplanned activity, carbohydrate about every 30 min. A low during exercise on closed loop may need less carbohydrate to treat (for example 10 g), though this is individual.
- Pump suspension: may suit high-impact or contact sports and shorter activity, but the closed loop must be switched off or it will count insulin as delivered. Avoid suspensions over 90–120 min unless insulin is replaced, for example by reconnecting every hour or using a pen; one option is about 50% of the usual hourly basal each hour.
- Switching to manual mode: 50–80% basal reduction from 90 min before until the end of exercise.
- After exercise, systems usually handle mild highs once returned to normal mode; with extreme highs (above 15.0 mmol/L, 270 mg/dL) a small correction, for example 50% of the usual dose, may be needed.

## Unplanned exercise

[Section 7.7, p. 1358; Section 8.8, p. 1360]
- Short play (under 15 min, such as a trampoline or school break) rarely causes lows. Beyond 15 min, fast carbohydrate is likely needed: in one study half of the young people dropped more than 2 mmol/L (36 mg/dL) between 15 and 30 min of walking. Use the Table 4/5 amounts for unplanned activity lasting about 20 min, including PE lessons and camps.
- On HCL: a snack 5–10 min before, carbohydrate about every 30 min, and a 25% bolus cut with the post-exercise meal if lows occur.

## Competition and athletes

[Sections 4.1, 9.1–9.2, p. 1344, 1361]
- Mild to moderate hyperglycaemia does not clearly harm performance (in one study at 17 mmol/L, peak oxygen uptake was only slightly lower), while even mild hypoglycaemia worsens reaction time and performance. Hypoglycaemia prevention comes first.
- Competition stress hyperglycaemia: allow more preparation time, check early, avoid over-fuelling. Pump users can raise basal temporarily but should return to baseline or lower shortly before the start. HCL users may delay exercise mode so the system keeps correcting. A light aerobic warm-up may help. Aggressively correcting pre-competition highs increases the risk of later lows.

## Contraindications

- Temporary contraindications: severe hypoglycaemia in the past 24 h; hyperglycaemia ≥15.0 mmol/L (270 mg/dL) with ketones from insulin deficiency; injury or acute infection. [Section 10.1, p. 1363]
- Unstable diabetes, frequent severe hypoglycaemia or recurrent ketoacidosis, or advanced complications: reduce or stop vigorous exercise until stable (C); high-intensity exercise is generally contraindicated with advanced or proliferative retinopathy. [p. 1342]

## Limits of this summary

- The chapter covers children and adolescents with T1D and T2D, but most insulin and carbohydrate numbers come from small studies in mostly male adults on treadmills or bikes; the tables are level D (consensus) starting points.
- Individual responses vary widely; plans must be tested and adjusted. HCL/AID guidance is largely expert opinion, and DIY systems (Trio, Loop, AndroidAPS) are only mentioned in general.
- Left out or shortened: physiology detail, competition-day and athlete detail beyond the basics, scuba diving and twice-daily regimens, weight-banded appendix tables (A1–A2), the full meal examples in Table 6, T2D behaviour-change sections, schools and limited-resource settings.
- Read the original (tables 2–9) before making clinical decisions.
