ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Assessment and management of hypoglycemia in children and adolescents with diabetes. Pediatr Diabetes. 2022;23(8):1322-1340, https://doi.org/10.1111/pedi.13443.*

## Key points

- Three levels of hypoglycaemia: level 1 "clinical alert" below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL), level 2 "clinically important or serious" below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL), level 3 "severe" (no glucose threshold; defined by severe cognitive impairment needing another person's help). Both level 1 and level 2 need treatment [Table 1; Executive summary, grade E].
- CGM targets for children with T1D: under 4% of time (under about 1 h/day) below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) and under 1% (under about 15 min/day) below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL) [Table 1, grade E].
- Treat with oral glucose. The aim is a rise of roughly 3–4 mmol/L (54–72 mg/dL), which needs about **0.3 g/kg** of glucose: about 9 g for a 30 kg child and 15 g for a child over 50 kg [Executive summary, grade C].
- On automated insulin delivery (AID, hybrid closed loop, predictive suspend), standard amounts can cause rebound hyperglycaemia; consider smaller amounts, e.g. **5–10 g**, or about half the standard amount [Executive summary, grade E; Hypoglycemia treatment].
- Recheck glucose **15 minutes** after treatment; if there is no or too little response, treat again and recheck after another 15 min [Executive summary, grade E].
- With a CGM, do not repeat treatment only because the sensor still reads low at 15 min: sensor lag means a fingerstick should confirm a persisting low first [Hypoglycemia treatment].
- On a standard pump (no automatic suspend or AID) with glucose below 3 mmol/L (54 mg/dL), stop insulin delivery until glucose is above 4 mmol/L (72 mg/dL) [Executive summary, grade E].
- Severe hypoglycaemia: glucagon 1 mg SC/IM for children over 25 kg, 0.5 mg under 25 kg; nasal glucagon 3 mg for children over 4 years; other ready-to-use forms are described [Table 4, grade A]. In hospital, IV 10% dextrose 2 mL/kg [grade B].
- Exercise can cause hypoglycaemia during activity or later; risk is raised during moderate exercise, right after it, and again **7–11 h** afterwards [Risk factors: Exercise; grade B].
- Monitor glucose overnight, especially when there is an extra risk factor for nocturnal hypoglycaemia [Executive summary, grade E].
- CGM, low-glucose suspend, predictive low-glucose suspend and hybrid closed-loop systems all reduce time in hypoglycaemia [Prevention, grade A].

## Definitions and levels of hypoglycemia

Hypoglycaemia is any episode with glucose low enough to cause symptoms or signs (including impaired brain function) and to expose the person to harm. The guideline says no single number fits everyone, but sets thresholds for care and reporting, based on SMBG, CGM or laboratory glucose [Definition].

| Level | Threshold | What it means | Acceptable CGM time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clinical hypoglycemia alert | <3.9 mmol/L (<70 mg/dL) | Treat; glucose may keep falling | <4% (<1 h/day) |
| Clinically important / serious | <3.0 mmol/L (<54 mg/dL) | Treat; neurogenic symptoms and cognitive dysfunction can start; record in audits | <1% (<15 min/day) |
| Severe hypoglycemia | No set threshold | Coma, convulsions or severe cognitive impairment; needs a third person to give carbohydrate, glucagon or IV dextrose | – |

[Table 1]

- The 3.9 mmol/L alert is used as the treatment threshold in children because of the chance of a further drop [Definition 1].
- Values below 3.0 mmol/L can lead to defective counter-regulation and impaired awareness [Definition 2].
- Hypoglycaemic coma (seizure or loss of consciousness) is a subgroup of severe hypoglycaemia and should be recorded separately. Because small children need help even for mild lows, the carer and clinician must judge whether cognitive impairment was present [Definition 3].
- Coma rates have fallen to about 3–7 per 100 patient-years in registries from high-income countries; low HbA1c is no longer a clear risk factor with modern intensive therapy [Incidence; grade B].

## Signs and symptoms

- **Autonomic (adrenergic):** shaking, sweating, trembling, palpitations, pallor.
- **Neuroglycopenic:** poor concentration, blurred or double vision, disturbed colour vision, hearing difficulty, slurred speech, confusion and poor judgement, short-term memory problems, weakness, numbness, dizziness, unsteady gait, loss of consciousness, seizure.
- **Behavioural:** irritability, erratic behaviour, agitation, nightmares, inconsolable crying.
- **Non-specific:** hunger, headache, nausea, tiredness. [Table 2]

In young (preschool) children behaviour changes such as tantrums, quietness or stubbornness may be the main sign, and observed signs matter more than reported symptoms. Neuroglycopenic symptoms are more common than autonomic ones in the young [Signs and symptoms]. In people without diabetes, symptoms start at about 3.9 mmol/L in children and adolescents versus 3.2 mmol/L in adults; chronic high glucose shifts the threshold up (one study: 4.9 mmol/L in adolescents with high glucose), repeated lows shift it down [Signs and symptoms; Physiological responses; grade B].

## Causes and risk factors

- Main cause: mismatch between insulin and food. Precipitants: too much insulin, less food or a missed meal, exercise, sleep, alcohol [Table 3; grade B].
- Risk factors: impaired awareness of hypoglycaemia, earlier severe hypoglycaemia, longer diabetes duration, coeliac disease, Addison's disease, hypothyroidism, psychological distress [Table 3].
- After a severe episode, the risk of another stays higher for up to 4 years. If unexplained lows are frequent, consider coeliac and Addison's disease; covert insulin use is rare but should be thought of as a sign of distress [Recurrent hypoglycemia; grade E].

## Exercise and hypoglycemia

The glucose effect of exercise depends on type, intensity, duration, time of day, starting glucose, insulin levels and carbohydrate available. Risk rises during moderate-intensity exercise, straight after, and 7–11 h later (delayed, often at night). Reasons: faster insulin absorption, higher insulin sensitivity, glycogen depletion and blunted counter-regulation. Children on fixed insulin doses face a "triple" risk on the night after exercise (glucose use, weaker counter-regulation in sleep, unchanged insulin) [Risk factors: Exercise]. Glucose should be checked before exercise, and extra carbohydrate may be taken based on the reading and the expected intensity and duration [Prevention; grade B]. Education on insulin changes for exercise should be given; details are in the ISPAD exercise chapter [Executive summary].

## Alcohol

Alcohol blocks gluconeogenesis, so hypoglycaemia can follow if little carbohydrate is eaten. Intoxication can hide hypo symptoms, and even moderate drinking may lower awareness and counter-regulation. Evening drinking also lowers night-time growth hormone and may raise the risk of a low the next morning. Whether alcohol raises insulin sensitivity is not settled [Risk factors: Alcohol]. Alcohol is listed as a precipitant particularly in adolescents [Executive summary].

## Nocturnal hypoglycemia

- In a 2010 CGM study, prolonged lows at night occurred on 8.5% of nights; mean time below 60 mg/dL (3.3 mmol/L) was 81 min, and about half of episodes went unnoticed [Nocturnal hypoglycemia].
- Sleep weakens counter-regulation and people with T1D rarely wake from a low; long night lows can cause seizures.
- Suspect it when the morning glucose is low, or with night-time confusion, nightmares or seizures, or with headache, lethargy, low mood or poor thinking on waking.
- No bedtime value reliably predicts a safe night. One CGM study found night lows in 45% vs 22% when bedtime glucose was ≤5.5 mmol/L (100 mg/dL) [Nocturnal hypoglycemia].
- Bedtime snacks were advised in the era of NPH insulin; with long-acting analogues they are not mandatory and should be individualised, as they can cause night highs and weight gain.
- Night lows are less common on pumps and fall further with suspend-on-low, suspend-before-low and hybrid closed-loop systems.
- Risk factors for night lows: younger age, lower HbA1c, exercise earlier in the day and an earlier low.

## Treatment of hypoglycemia: amounts and choice of carbohydrate

**When to treat.** Detect with SMBG or CGM. Factory-calibrated CGMs are approved for treatment decisions, but a fingerstick is advised when the sensor value does not match what is expected, and glucose should always be measured if the child has symptoms or signs [Hypoglycemia treatment; grade B]. Below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL), act to prevent a further fall.

**How much.**
- In adults, 20 g glucose (tablets) raised glucose by about 2.5–3.6 mmol/L (45–65 mg/dL) at 45 min. This was scaled to **0.3 g/kg** for children (about 9 g at 30 kg, 15 g at 50 kg) [Hypoglycemia treatment].
- A paediatric study found 0.3 g/kg of fast carbohydrate (glucose tablets or orange juice) resolved most lows, raising median glucose by 1–1.3 mmol/L at 10 min and 2–2.1 mmol/L at 15 min, with no rebound high at the next meal. A weight-based dose also worked on pumps.
- **On AID / closed loop:** no studies yet define the right amount. Because the system has usually already cut or stopped insulin, the standard amount can cause rebound highs; consider less glucose, **5–10 g**, or roughly half the standard amount [Hypoglycemia treatment; grade E].
- The amount also depends on body size, type of insulin therapy, active **insulin on board**, and the timing and intensity of recent exercise. Families should think about what caused the low.

**Which carbohydrate.**
- Glucose products are first choice; check how much glucose the product holds. Glucose tablets relieve symptoms at 15 min more often than dietary sugars (hard candy, table sugar, jelly beans, juice) [Hypoglycemia treatment].
- Doses for dietary sugars are not established and may need to be higher: about 40 g of carbohydrate as juice gives roughly the rise of 20 g of glucose tablets; sucrose also needs more than glucose; fructose-based juice was less effective in one study. Honey's sugar mix varies by origin.
- Jelly beans raise glucose but resolve the low more slowly. Soft drinks must be regular, not diet.
- Avoid fat-containing foods (chocolate) and complex carbohydrates as first treatment: they slow absorption. Whole milk with 20 g carbohydrate (about 435 mL) raised glucose by only about 1 mmol/L (18 mg/dL).
- Sweets as treatment are discouraged so that "treatment" is not confused with "treats".

**Recheck and follow-up.**
- Recheck glucose after 15 min; if the response is absent or too small, give the same amount again, then recheck again after 15 min to confirm the target is reached [grade E].
- Because of the physiological **lag of CGM**, a still-low sensor at 15 min should be confirmed by fingerstick before re-treating.
- After recovery, eat the usual meal or snack if it is due; otherwise a snack of about 15 g slower carbohydrate (bread, milk, biscuits, fruit). This is often not needed on pump therapy [Hypoglycemia treatment].
- On a standard pump without automatic suspend, suspend basal insulin if glucose is below 3 mmol/L until it is above 4 mmol/L [grade E].

## Severe hypoglycemia and glucagon

If the child is unconscious or cannot swallow, give glucagon (IV, nasal, IM or SC) [Severe hypoglycemia].

| Product | Route | Dose | Age |
|---|---|---|---|
| Glucagon powder 1 mg/mL (reconstituted) | SC/IM | 1 mg if >25 kg; 0.5 mg if <25 kg | all ages |
| Nasal glucagon (Baqsimi) | intranasal | 3 mg | >4 years |
| Dasiglucagon | SC | 0.6 mg | ≥6 years |
| Gvoke HypoPen | SC | 1 mg if >45 kg; 0.5 mg if <45 kg | >2 years |

[Table 4]

- Novo Nordisk uses a 25 kg cut-off for 0.5 vs 1 mg, Eli Lilly 20 kg; the evidence for either is unclear.
- Glucagon often causes nausea and vomiting on waking; keep observing and monitoring. It works less well when liver glycogen is low (e.g. after long fasting), where IV glucose is preferred.
- Nasal glucagon was as effective as SC/IM glucagon in a meta-analysis, is faster to give and has fewer dosing errors; side effects include headache, nasal discomfort, watery eyes.
- Glucagon should be available to all parents and carers, with training on how to use it [grade E].
- Hospital: IV glucose 0.2 g/kg (2 mL/kg of 10% dextrose), maximum 0.5 g/kg (5 mL/kg), given over several minutes; avoid 50% dextrose (osmotic brain injury risk). For recurrent lows, 10% dextrose infusion at 2–5 mg/kg/min (1.2–3.0 mL/kg/h).
- Where glucagon is unavailable: do not force liquids or powders into a semi-conscious child; place on the side and smear a thick glucose paste on the inside of the lower cheek (efficacy anecdotal).
- After a severe episode the risk of another is higher and insulin changes may be needed.

**Mini-dose glucagon.** For children with gastrointestinal illness or poor intake and glucose ≤4.4 mmol/L (80 mg/dL), carers can inject small SC doses with an insulin syringe (1 unit ≈ 10 μg): 2 units (20 μg) for age ≤2 years, then 1 unit per year of age for 3–15 years, maximum 15 units (150 μg). If glucose has not risen after 30 min, give double the first dose. This raised glucose by 3.3–5 mmol/L (60–90 mg/dL) within 30 min [Minidose glucagon].

## Impaired hypoglycemia awareness

- Glucagon response to lows is lost early (from about 12 months after onset, in most people by 5 years), so adrenaline becomes the main defence. Repeated lows blunt counter-regulation further ("hypoglycaemia begets hypoglycaemia") [Hypoglycemia awareness].
- Impaired awareness affects about 25% of adults with T1D; in children it fell from 33% (2002) to 21% (2015). It raises severe hypoglycaemia about sixfold.
- Antecedent hypoglycaemia, moderate exercise and sleep all lower the response to a following low.
- Screening tools: Gold score (one question), Clarke (eight questions), modified Clarke (six questions); a score of 4 or more suggests impaired awareness. Awareness is a continuum, not all-or-none [Appendices].
- Awareness can recover after 2–3 weeks of strict hypo avoidance; CGM, suspend features and closed loop may help [grade B].
- Families should contact their team if lows occur without symptoms or with only neuroglycopenic symptoms [grade E]. Glucose targets may need to be raised for children with recurrent lows or impaired awareness [grade B].

## CGM alerts, pumps and closed loop

- Pump therapy (CSII) is linked to fewer severe lows than injections in registries [Technology].
- CGM lowers time in hypoglycaemia; **predictive alerts** reduce it further and may reduce parents' fear of hypoglycaemia. Real-time CGM reduces lows more than intermittently scanned CGM without alerts, so is better for people at high risk.
- Predictive low-glucose suspend: Medtronic PLGM suspends basal when sensor glucose is within 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) above the set low limit and predicted to be 1.1 mmol/L (20 mg/dL) above it in 30 min; it resumes after at most 2 h. Tandem Basal-IQ suspends when glucose is predicted to be 4.4 mmol/L (80 mg/dL) in 30 min or is below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL). Both cut time low (about 50% and 31% relative reduction in the cited studies).
- Hybrid closed-loop systems (Medtronic 670G/770G/780G, Control-IQ, CamAPS FX) raise and lower insulin automatically from sensor glucose but still need meal boluses. They consistently reduce time low and increase time in range, especially overnight, and may help awareness [Hybrid closed-loop systems].

## Fear of hypoglycemia and prevention

Fear of hypoglycaemia can lead families or clinicians to accept higher glucose levels. Its strongest predictor for parents is having seen a severe event. Regular screening (e.g. Hypoglycemia Fear Survey, Children's Hypoglycemia Index) is advised [grade E]. Prevention rests on education for children, parents, teachers and carers about early signs and immediate treatment; a glucose meter and fast glucose must always be available; children should wear diabetes identification [Prevention of hypoglycemia; grades A–E].

## Limits of this summary

- Written for children and adolescents with diabetes; adult thresholds are mentioned only for comparison.
- Most treatment amounts are grade C or E (expert consensus); the AID amount (5–10 g) is explicitly not based on trials.
- Mortality, long-term brain effects, incidence data and full questionnaire wording are left out or shortened.
- For clinical decisions, read the original guideline and follow the care team's plan.
